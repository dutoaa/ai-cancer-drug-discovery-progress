CoRe-WM: Conditioned Response-State World Modeling for Longitudinal Treatment-Response Prediction in Breast DCE-MRI

Lin Zhao, Bowen Chen, Qiaohui Gao, Shaowen Wan, Xiang Li, Wei Liu

Pathologic complete response (pCR) after neoadjuvant chemotherapy is a key prognostic indicator in aggressive breast cancer, yet existing prediction approaches either rely on pretreatment imaging alone, missing the patient's evolving response, or optimize multi-visit classifiers under direct outcome supervision, coupling representation learning with the clinical endpoint. We formulate longitudinal pCR prediction as a world-modeling problem and propose Conditioned Response-state World Modeling (CoRe-WM), which learns a latent state-transition model that predicts the next imaging state from the observed sequence. The transition function is conditioned on patient clinical profile and assigned therapy through additive shift and feature-wise modulation, enabling patient- and treatment-specific dynamics. A Factorized Response State pathway further decouples treatment-response dynamics from imaging appearance by encoding the structural prior that response manifests primarily in lesion morphology. After pretraining, all parameters are frozen and a linear readout predicts pCR at each clinical decision point. We evaluate CoRe-WM on I-SPY2 under five-fold cross-validation and assess cross-cohort transfer on I-SPY1. CoRe-WM achieves the highest discrimination among all evaluated methods at every prospective decision point, and demonstrates robust cross-cohort transfer. Ablation studies identify patient-profile conditioning and the factorized response pathway as the principal contributors. These results establish world-model-based representation learning as a promising framework for outcome prediction in oncology and beyond.

Generative Retrieval for Reverse Target Fishing with a Proteome-Scale Benchmark

Zhichao Deng, Haichuan Tan, Xin Hong, Huaizhi Wang, Tao Huang, Bowen Gao

Reverse target fishing identifies protein targets for a query molecule and is central to drug discovery and repurposing. Discriminative approaches adapted from virtual screening face sparse, long-tailed associations and missing-not-at-random measurements: treating unrecorded pairs as negatives can suppress genuine targets. We reformulate the task as generative retrieval and introduce GenFish. Residual vector quantization converts frozen protein language model features into shared semantic identifiers. Given a molecular fingerprint, GenFish autoregressively models codes conditioned on the query and preceding code, ranking proteins by identifier likelihood without explicitly labeling unrecorded pairs as negatives. We introduce ProTargetBench, the first proteome-scale benchmark for reverse target fishing to jointly support measured-panel discrimination and full-catalogue recovery of experimentally recorded positives. Covering 20,431 UniProt-reviewed human proteins, it integrates curated bioactivity evidence and distinguishes experimental inactivity from unmeasured associations. On 198 held-out molecules, GenFish achieves higher mean performance across all three full-catalogue retrieval metrics than four discriminative neural baselines and three non-neural baselines, including ligand-based methods. A controlled positive-hiding experiment shows that targeted removal of false-negative competition improves contrastive retrieval beyond matched random masking; GenFish remains stronger under the same incomplete supervision. Pfam alignment indicates that the semantic identifiers capture biologically meaningful relationships among proteins. These results establish generative retrieval as a principled paradigm for reverse target fishing under incomplete bioactivity records.

From Adult Renal Masses to Pediatric Wilms Tumors: Evaluating Cross-Domain Generalization of Kidney Lesion Segmentation on MRI

Sarah de Boer, Myrthe A. D. Buser, Hartmut Häntze, Jawed Nawabi, Lisa C. Adams, Alida F. W. van der Steeg

Deep learning segmentation models for pediatric oncology are often trained on small, single-center cohorts, whereas much larger datasets exist for adult diseases, such as kidney cancer. Whether models trained on adult renal lesions generalize to pediatric Wilms tumors remains unclear. We trained a segmentation model on single-sequence T1- or T2-weighted adult kidney MRI and evaluated it on a held-out adult test set and a pediatric Wilms tumor cohort, where it was compared with a pediatric-specific nnU-Net. Segmentation performance was quantified using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) and 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance. On the adult test set, the model achieved a median DSC of 0.86 for renal lesions. On the pediatric cohort, the adult-trained model achieved a median DSC of 0.85 compared with 0.90 for the pediatric-specific model. Despite differences in age, anatomy, and tumor type, the adult-trained model showed promising performance on pediatric Wilms tumors, with only a modest difference in median DSC compared with the pediatric-specific model. However, non-inferiority could not be shown, highlighting the value of pediatric training data. In future work, a fine-tuning strategy could be explored.

PRET is a few-shot system for pan-cancer recognition without example training

Yi Li, Ziyu Ning, Tianqi Xiang, Qixiang Zhang, Zhihao Lin, Min Yi

Pathological examination stands as the cornerstone in cancer diagnosis, impacting millions worldwide annually. With the shortage of pathologists globally, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged rapidly to automate the diagnostics process. However, conventional AI models require substantial labeled data for each disease, posing huge challenges in scalability and practicality. Therefore, we introduce PRET (pan-cancer recognition without examples training), a few-shot system to achieve flexible, scalable, and effective cancer recognition across diverse organs, hospitals and tasks without training. Evaluated on 23 international benchmarks comprising 4,484 whole-slide images, our method outperforms existing approaches across 20 tasks, achieving over 97% area under the curve on 15 benchmarks with a maximum improvement of 36.76%. Notably, PRET delivers clinical-grade diagnostic performance in lymph node metastasis detection using only eight slide examples, outperforming 11 pathologists. By offering a flexible and cost-effective solution for pan-cancer recognition, PRET paves the way for accessible and equitable AI-based pathology systems, particularly benefiting minority populations and underserved regions.

Multi-scale Fusion of Fast Vision Mamba and Foundation Model Features for Patch-Level Gleason Grading in Computational Pathology

Zihe Zhang, Weimiao Yu, Piumi Don Simonge

Automated Gleason grading of prostate whole slide images (WSIs) enables faster and more consistent cancer risk stratification. However, it remains a challenging task due to the subtle morphological differences between grades, the severe class imbalance arising from rare patterns, and the diagnostic features that span multiple spatial scales. Pathology foundation models (FMs) extract strong patch representations but often operate at a single-scale, which limits spatial context and disproportionately affects classes that depend on broader context. The challenge of fusing multiple scales and complementary models, with minimal data and computational resources, remains unresolved. We propose a multi-scale classifier built on state-space models. Our method combines two complementary feature sources: a frozen foundation model, and a lightweight FastVim, pre-trained on ImageNet and distilled from the foundation model, that reads the raw image crops. At each scale, a Mamba model encodes the features from both sources with linear-time spatial context, and a jointly trained head fuses the encoded representations into one prediction. On the PANDA dataset, our method reaches macro F1 of 0.894 and Gleason 5 F1 of 0.802 under 5-fold cross-validation, a 12% and 21% relative improvement over the best single-scale model. A controlled study over 5 foundation models, 5 base classifiers and 3 scales confirms that the gain holds across encoders, with larger improvements observed at smaller scales.

Multimodal-Conditioned Latent Diffusion Models for Spatiotemporal Brain MRI Forecasting

Antonio Scardace, Silvia Ingala, Sebastiano Battiato, Daniele Ravì

Spatiotemporal forecasting of 3D anatomical changes is a critical generative vision challenge for modeling Alzheimer's Disease (AD) progression. While brain MRI forecasting from single-modality data has advanced, other key complementary multimodal biomarkers remain underutilized. In this work, we propose the Multimodal-Conditioned Latent Diffusion Model (MC-LDM), a novel framework for patient-specific brain MRI synthesis. MC-LDM leverages multimodal biomarkers---MRI, tau-PET, Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR), and APOE4---during training, but relies only on baseline MRI, APOE4, and patient metadata at inference, reflecting realistic clinical availability. Our contributions are threefold: (1) a Latent Diffusion Model (LDM) fine-tuned via Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for data-efficient learning; (2) a multimodal conditioning that integrates molecular and clinical signals to model heterogeneous disease trajectories; and (3) auxiliary predictors estimating unobserved multimodal biomarkers from MRI features and patient covariates to condition the diffusion model, removing the need for these assessments at inference. Trained on 1,355 visits from 852 subjects in the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), MC-LDM reduces average regional volumetric MAE by 18% over the second-best baseline on an independent test set of 664 visits. To our knowledge, MC-LDM represents the first diffusion-based framework to leverage multimodal priors for MRI forecasting. This conceptual advancement enables patient-specific trajectory generation that captures aspects of AD heterogeneity not observable from structural anatomy alone, bridging the gap between rich multimodal supervision and the strict constraints of routine clinical deployment. Code is available at: https://github.com/antonioscardace/MC-LDM.

Quantifying Cancer Clinical Trial Eligibility Using Artificial Intelligence-Based Matching

Goel, K. P.; Myall, N. J.; Dickerson, J.; Caswell-Jin, J. L.; Johnson, T.; Worth, J. E.; Gensheimer, M. F.

PURPOSETo develop and validate an artificial intelligence-enabled platform that combines efficient screening with explicit eligibility logic by converting unstructured cancer trial criteria into reusable structured queries, and illustrates potential eligibility using synthetic patients. METHODSWe downloaded actively recruiting US interventional treatment trials for advanced/metastatic breast cancer, colon cancer, and non-small cell lung cancer from ClinicalTrials.gov. Medical oncologists created 24 synthetic patient vignettes. A large language model converted trial eligibility criteria into Structured Query Language (SQL) code and patient information into structured records, enabling rule-based matching without further LLM inference. Cancer details and treatment history were considered, but not laboratory results or comorbidities. Validation included physician editing of generated eligibility code for 30 trials and independent assessments by two physicians, followed by consensus adjudication, for eligibility across ten trials. We descriptively summarized potential eligibility by trial characteristics and when varying age, ECOG performance status, sex, and ZIP code. RESULTSOf 833 candidate trials, 746 met inclusion criteria. Performing matching for all 746 trials took a median of 0.459 seconds per patient, excluding patient-data preparation. Against physician consensus, automated matching agreed on 230/240 patient-trial pairs (95.8%; sensitivity 83.3%, specificity 100%). Across synthetic patients, potentially eligible trials ranged from 10 to 258 without geographic restrictions. Counts decreased with worse performance status and geographic restriction and were relatively stable across ages 30-90. Later-phase, randomized, and molecularly selective trials generally matched fewer vignettes. CONCLUSIONLLM-generated SQL enables rapid, explicit rule-based screening for potential trial eligibility. Evaluation in synthetic vignettes supports feasibility, while missed matches underscore the need for further validation.

What Language Adds, and at What Cost: Four Controlled Probes of Pathology Vision-Language Models

Wenhao Zhang, Zhongliang Zhou, Yi Ping, Zirui Wang, Sheng Li

Vision--language models (VLMs) promise to make computational pathology queryable and able to draft reports, but how much language adds beyond the vision backbone is unclear, because almost every system is evaluated by its developers on test sets of their own choosing. We grade published evidence by how it was produced and test four claims with controlled probes that reuse released checkpoints and public data, varying one input at a time. Report text adds prognostic signal: adding a frozen embedding of the stage-redacted report to a slide embedding raises the pooled C-index by 0.019 for progression-free interval and by 0.016 for overall survival across 1{,}633 TCGA patients, and restoring the stage statement adds nothing. Prompts add no measurable accuracy at inference: with features and pooling fixed, zero-shot classification is statistically indistinguishable from a supervised linear probe for lung and renal cancer subtyping. Slide-level question answering is barely grounded: SlideChat answers 74.82\% of SlideBench-VQA(TCGA) items correctly with the slide and 74.41\% without it, whereas its language backbone alone reaches 43.52\%. Paraphrasing leaves accuracy unchanged but flips 8--23\% of answers. Self-reported gains from agentic navigation and few-shot language priors cannot yet be attributed to language. Language thus adds real but narrow value, and we propose minimum controls that would expose these failure modes in future evaluations.

MCDA: Multi-View Graph Contrastive Learning with Dynamic Topology Augmentation for Functional MRI Analysis

Hao Zhang, Xiaoyun Liu, Shuo Huang, Yue Ma, Liang Cao, Mingxia Liu

Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) is a vital non-invasive tool for diagnosing brain disorders and uncovering pathological mechanisms. However, existing graph contrastive learning (GCL) methods often underexploit augmentation histories across training epochs and the relationships among different augmented views. In this paper, we introduce a topology-guided Multi-view graph Contrastive learning framework with Dynamic graph topology Augmentation (MCDA) for fMRI analysis. Unlike traditional GCL that uses fixed noise or edge-dropping, MCDA employs a Dynamic Augmentation Update (DAU) module that iteratively refines the augmented topology. Specifically, we incorporate self-aware and cross-sample augmentation strategies to simultaneously capture individual-specific functional signatures and inter-subject heterogeneity. We then design a topology-guided multi-view graph contrastive learning scheme that explores interrelationships not only between the original network and its augmented views, but also across different augmented views to maximize structural information preservation. We characterize the score-level optimization directions and establish a lower bound for the contrastive objective. The framework combines classification and contrastive losses for brain disorder diagnosis and examines discriminative functional connectivity patterns as potential biomarkers. Experiments on two public rs-fMRI datasets comprising 1,236 subjects demonstrate that MCDA outperforms the state-of-the-art methods. The code has been made publicly available at: \url{https://github.com/goodcodingagoodboy/MCDA}.

Arbitrary-Scale Anisotropic 3D MRI Super-Resolution with Gaussian Prototypes

Aditya Rastogi, Ramon Leiser, Prateek Singh, Martha Foltyn-Dumitru, Martin Bendszus, Fabian Bamberg

Reducing MRI acquisition time improves patient comfort, reduces motion artefacts, and increases scanner throughput. Clinical protocols often shorten acquisitions by sacrificing spatial resolution, particularly along the slice axis. Super-resolution can recover higher-resolution images, but existing methods are typically specialised to one scale or acquisition geometry, limiting their use across heterogeneous clinical protocols. We present GSMR3D, a continuous volumetric model that reconstructs arbitrary isotropic and anisotropic resolutions with a single checkpoint per MRI contrast. It combines a learned dictionary of anisotropic 3D Gaussian prototypes with scale-conditioned frequency encoding and an exact measurement-consistency projection, keeping reconstructions consistent with the acquired low-resolution data. We train separate models on 3,731 contrast-enhanced T1-weighted and 30,254 T1-weighted brain MRI volumes from clinical and population-based cohorts. We evaluate GSMR3D against geometry-specific specialists for isotropic, in-plane, and through-plane super-resolution using reconstruction quality and downstream tasks: brain tumour segmentation, brain-metastasis detection, and brain parcellation. GSMR3D outperforms specialists at isotropic $2\times$ and achieves better LPIPS, DISTS, and FID across all geometries, although specialists retain higher PSNR in anisotropic settings. It more faithfully preserves glioma segmentations across all geometries, achieves the highest lesion sensitivity on an independent brain-metastasis cohort, and the highest brain-parcellation agreement for isotropic and in-plane reconstruction. Reconstruction takes about 6s per volume, adding little overhead relative to acquisition-time savings. GSMR3D offers a flexible approach to MRI scan-time reduction with one model per contrast across geometries while preserving information relevant to downstream tasks.

DR-Learners for Generalizing CATEs from a Randomized Controlled Trial

Vanessa Rodriguez, Brieuc Lehmann, Karla Diaz-Ordaz

Randomized clinical trials are essential for evaluating interventions, yet average treatment effects can obscure treatment effect heterogeneity, making conditional average treatment effects (CATEs) a natural target. However, restrictive eligibility criteria and differential trial participation can limit the generalizability of these effects to broader target populations. Building on existing DR-Learners, we develop the gDR-Learner for generalizing RCT CATEs to a target population, using cross-fitted machine-learning nuisance estimation. We derive excess-risk bounds in which nuisance errors enter only as products, showing that the estimator achieves oracle-rate convergence despite slower individual nuisance estimation. The variables needed to generalize are rarely measured across the entire target population. We therefore extend the methods to two settings with incomplete covariate information: when certain covariates are measured only in the trial (e.g., biomarkers), and covariates driving trial participation measured only in a subsample of the target population, such as a representative cohort or registry. We construct weighted conformal prediction intervals for individual treatment effects, accounting for covariate shift between the trial and target population. In simulations, the gDR-Learner achieved the lowest estimation error when all covariates were observed relative to standard T- and DR-Learners and an inverse-probability-weighted T-Learner, and also outperformed the latter under incomplete covariate observation. We illustrate the methods by reanalyzing the Coronary Artery Surgery Study, examining treatment effect heterogeneity by prior myocardial infarction and baseline ejection fraction.

On the Selectivity of Generative Models in Structure-Based Drug Design

Ella Miray Rajaonson, Jungyoon Lee, William Chau, Alan Aspuru-Guzik, Benjamin Sanchez-Lengeling, Dominique Beaini

Designing small-molecule drugs that selectively bind to a target protein while avoiding unintended interactions with other proteins is essential for reducing side effects that contribute substantially to clinical attrition. Although structure-based drug design (SBDD) has advanced rapidly with deep learning, most generative models still optimize binding to a single target and disregard selectivity due to limited training data and unknown off-target interactions. Motivated by this gap, we introduce SELECTBENCH, a comprehensive benchmark assessing the selectivity of generative SBDD models. SELECTBENCH provides three curated evaluation tasks reflecting different real-world drug discovery scenarios: literature-based case studies, safety screening panels used in pharmaceutical development, and large-scale structure-based evaluation sets. We further introduce a suite of metrics to quantify selectivity and conduct a systematic evaluation of nine representative SBDD models, including methods explicitly trained for selectivity and widely-used inference-time guidance techniques. Our results show that even selectivity-trained models achieve only modest off-target discrimination and inference-time guidance provides limited gains, underscoring the need for method improvement. SELECTBENCH provides both a characterization of where current generative SBDD stands on selectivity and an open, extensible platform to accelerate progress. The benchmark is available at https://github.com/lovetaro/selectbench.

PRISM: Prior Relational Information for Self-supervised Modeling to Enhance Solubility OOD Generalization

Xinyi Chen, Jiahuan Pang, Titus Chima, Paul Weng, Wendong Wang

Solubility depends on intermolecular interactions between solutes and solvents, and is central to drug discovery, chemical and materials process engineering. Its prediction requires out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization to unseen solutes, solvents, and solvent mixtures, where supervised methods suffer from data scarcity, and existing self-supervised methods do not explicitly learn chemically meaningful relations. Learning chemically meaningful relational information without labels can alleviate data scarcity and improve OOD generalization, but remains challenging. We introduce Prior Relational Information for Self-supervised Modeling (PRISM) and apply it to solubility OOD generalization. PRISM is a label-free pretraining framework that converts expert-defined chemical priors into relational teachers. For solubility prediction, it trains four encoders to preserve the population-level relational information from priors capturing hydrophobic, electrostatic, polarizability, and substructural effects, using Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence to match distance-based neighborhood distributions during pretraining. Across scaffold, solute, and solvent OOD settings, PRISM matches or surpasses supervised and self-supervised baselines. Under mixed-solvent OOD, freezing the four PRISM encoders and training a lightweight fusion-and-composition head outperforms strong baselines and substantially reduces run-to-run variation. Ablations and control studies confirm that gains stem from chemically meaningful relational structures baked into the encoders by PRISM. We expect this strategy of turning relational priors into self-supervision signals to extend beyond solubility prediction to domains where chemically or biologically meaningful relational priors are available.
