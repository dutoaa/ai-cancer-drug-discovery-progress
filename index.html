Integrating Histopathology Foundation Models and PRAME Immunohistochemistry in Melanoma Diagnosis

Gellrich, F. F.; Kistner, A.; Guenther, C.; Hufnagel, C.; Hobelsberger, S.; Laske, J.; Beissert, S.; Aust, D.; Baretton, G.; Reidow, N.; Sergon, M.; Kather, J. N.; Ghaffari Laleh, N.

BackgroundThe histopathological diagnosis of challenging melanocytic lesions remains limited by substantial interobserver variability. Although computational pathology foundation models demonstrated promising diagnostic performance, their predictions are often difficult to interpret in terms of established criteria. ObjectiveWe evaluated a diagnostic framework combining foundation-model-based whole-slide image analysis, automated prediction of morphological features, and manually assessed PRAME immunohistochemistry. MethodsUsing the Virchow2 foundation model and STAMP framework, we analyzed a retrospective cohort of 1,000 melanocytic lesions undergoing PRAME immunohistochemistry. Models were trained on 800 cases and evaluated on a temporally held-out test cohort of 200 cases. ResultsThe slide-level malignancy classifier achieved a test AUROC of 0.970 (AUPRC 0.984). Separate models predicted established histopathological criteria with high discrimination, including ulceration (AUROC 0.991) and dermal mitoses (AUROC 0.966). In contrast, predicting PRAME expression from H&E images alone showed lower performance (AUROC 0.776-0.780 for PRAME scores 4 and 0). An integrated model combining the slide-level malignancy score, AI-predicted morphological features, and manual PRAME status achieved an AUROC of 0.972 (AUPRC 0.975), comparable to the standalone classifier. In multivariable analysis, PRAME score 4 versus 0 was strongly associated with malignancy (adjusted OR 35.04, 95% CI 17.39-74.62). ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate the feasibility of combining foundation-model-based histopathological analysis with established morphological criteria and PRAME immunohistochemistry. Although the integrated framework did not substantially improve overall discrimination, it provides a structured approach for incorporating complementary histopathological and immunohistochemical information into AI-assisted assessment of melanocytic lesions. Plain Language SummaryDiagnosing melanoma, a type of skin cancer, under a microscope can be challenging. In difficult cases, doctors may sometimes reach different conclusions. Artificial intelligence (AI) computer programs can accurately analyze digitized tissue samples, but they often function as "black boxes," making it difficult for doctors to understand how the AI reached its conclusion. In this study, we tested an AI system on 1,000 skin samples. Instead of relying solely on the AI to provide a final diagnosis, we combined its analysis with specific microscopic features that doctors routinely look for, alongside the results of a special laboratory test called PRAME. We found that the AI system was highly accurate in identifying melanoma. While combining the AI with the specific tissue features and the PRAME test did not further increase the overall accuracy, it successfully aligned the computers output with established medical standards. This approach demonstrates that advanced computer models can be integrated with traditional laboratory tests. By linking AI results to familiar medical criteria, the systems output becomes more transparent for physicians, which could help doctors evaluate difficult skin tissue samples more efficiently in the future.

Sex and Gender Reporting and Female Participation Relative to Disease Burden in ClinicalTrials.gov

Green, M. D.; Aziz, M.; Sullenger, R. D.; Yoo, B. J.; O'Brien, E. C.; Navar, A. M.; Duroseau, B.; Thorpe, R. J.; Brantner, C. L.

ImportanceAssessing representation by sex assigned at birth and gender identity requires reporting of both in trials. If disclosure of full gender and sex participation from clinicaltrials.gov this is limited, it poses challenges in characterization and understanding of those participant experiences. ObjectiveTo determine how often ClinicalTrials.gov trials published trials report sex and gender, which characteristics are associated with reporting, and how participants female share compares with that of the general public. Design, Setting, and ParticipantsCross-sectional study of interventional trials registered to ClinicalTrials.gov, with or without US sites, with primary completion on or after September 27, 2008, and results first posted from January 1, 2009, through April 30, 2026. ExposuresFunder type, phase, US sites, enrollment, primary completion year, number of countries, and therapeutic area. Main Outcomes and MeasuresReporting of sex and gender, and the female participation-to-prevalence ratio (PPR; trial female share divided by the number of people with the condition). ResultsAmong 58,007 trials (median enrollment, 72 [IQR, 31 to 206]; 42,644 [74%] with US sites), 553 (0.95%) reported gender and 57,683 (99.44%) reported sex. Gender reporting was more common with National Institutes of Health (NIH) funding (odds ratio [OR], 9.05; 95% CI, 6.65 to 12.6), US sites (OR, 4.21; 95% CI, 2.96 to 6.22), and larger enrollment. Across 10 conditions (15,552 mixed-sex trials), median PPR ranged from 0.68 (95% CI, 0.64 to 0.71) for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease to 1.05 (95% CI, 1.01 to 1.09) for obesity and had a relatively lower female share in 8. Among the 10 conditions most prevalent in our cohort, 6,917 of 16,097 trial condition combinations (43.0%) reached a PPR of at least 0.90; odds of reaching it were higher for NIH-funded (OR, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.11 to 1.34) and lower for government-funded trials (OR, 0.26; 95% CI, 0.20 to 0.33) as compared to industry-funded trials. Conclusions and RelevanceIn ClinicalTrials.gov, sex reporting is nearly universal, whereas gender reporting is concentrated among NIH-funded, US-based, and larger trials. Female trial enrollment fell below the female share of people with the condition for most conditions, emphasizing the benefit of condition-specific benchmarks in clinical trials.

Deep Time-to-Event Models for Intrapartum Fetal Monitoring

Imai Aldeia, G. S.; Coggan, H.; Yang, Y.; Levine, L.; McCoy, J. A.; La Cava, W.

Timely detection of fetal distress during labor is a central pursuit of modern obstetric care. The primary measure used in this regard is fetal heart rate and contraction monitoring (echocar-diotocography or CTG), which provides a continuous signal during labor. The vast majority of machine learning applications to CTG assume access to a 30 to 60 minute window just prior to delivery, which makes an untenable assumption that the timing of delivery is known. In this work, we reframe the problem as a time-to-event prediction task. We develop deep time-to-event models to predict the joint probability of imminent delivery and fetal acidosis, a biomarker of fetal hypoxia collected from umbilical cord gas after delivery. We develop a framework for this problem, drawing on deep discrete-time survival methods and marked point processes, adapting each to this particular setting (i.e., continuous physiological signals, fully observed events, partially observed labels). One approach dubbed Marked DeepHit accurately estimates acidemic delivery within one hour at multiple elapsed-time land-marks, with test-set AUROC of 0.837 [95% CI 0.692-0.970] and 0.935 [0.858-0.964] at 6 and 12 hours into labor, respectively. We find that these models also achieve competitive performance near delivery time compared to prior models trained specifically on the end of tracing, and outperform other clinical feature-based proposals, both on internal and external validation at a second site. These results give a proof-of-principle to support prospective evaluation of AI-EFM during labor. Data and Code AvailabilityWe use data from a multi-site academic health system (University of Pennsylvania Health System) and the CTU-UHB repository (Chuda[c]ek et al., 2014). Access to CTU-UHB is openly available under the terms of the data use agreement on Physionet. Code for this paper is available at https://github.com/cavalab/deep-tte-intrapartum-fetal-monitoring. Institutional Review Board (IRB)This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at University of Pennsylvania under a waiver of informed consent.

MorphoStain: Physics-Guided Latent Diffusion and Morphological Distillation for Multiplex Stain Decomposition in Alzheimer's Neuropathology

Saraei, M.; Zand, M.; Rahmani, S.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) enables simultaneous biomarker profiling, yet unmixing colocalized targets with identical spectra, such as dual-DAB marking Tau and Amyloid-{beta} in Alzheimers pathology, remains mathematically ill-posed. We propose MorphoStain, an unsupervised, physics-guided framework for morphological stain decomposition with epistemic uncertainty quantification. MorphoStain models stain separation via a conditional Latent Diffusion Teacher operating on a Beer- Lambert optical-density manifold, guided by pathology foundation models. To enable real-time clinical throughput, the teacher is distilled into a FastStudentU-Net (0.12M parameters, > 1,400 x compression), while a Symmetric Morphological Routing Module dynamically disentangles identical chromogen overlap. Evaluated on 9,908 held-out patches from the SEA-AD cohort, MorphoStain achieves 40.56 dB PSNR and 0.9961 SSIM at 140.1 FPS, showing statistically significant gains over InverseAE (Cohens d = 3.52) and NMF (Cohens d = 7.02), with p < 0.001 for both comparisons.

Population-based reference equations and Z-scores for blood biomarkers of neurodegenerative diseases

Lehmann, S.; Andriambelosoa, T.; Morchikh, M.; Mortamais, M.; Duchiron, M.; Gabelle, A.; Hirtz, C.; Ayrignac, X.; Busto, G.; Bennys, K.; Kab, S.; Helmer, C.; Zins, M.; Helmer, C.; Mura, T.

BackgroundBlood biomarkers are increasingly used to support the diagnosis and monitoring of neurodegenerative diseases. However, their interpretation is complicated by physiological determinants, including age, sex, body-mass index, and renal function, and by differences in absolute concentrations between analytical methods. We aimed to develop population-based reference equations allowing individualized interpretation of selected blood biomarkers blood biomarkers used in neurology. MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we analysed plasma samples from cognitively unimpaired participants selected from the French CONSTANCES and Three-City population-based cohorts. Generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape were used to model neurofilament light chain (NfL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), phosphorylated tau 181 (p-tau181), amyloid-{beta}40, amyloid-{beta}42, and their ratios according to age, sex, body-mass index, and renal function. The resulting equations provided individualized expected concentrations, percentiles, and Z-scores. Previously established disease-specific concentrations were converted into Z-score. Cross-calibration equations were developed for NfL measurements across analytical methods and sample matrices. FindingsThe final reference populations comprised 5123 participants for amyloid biomarkers and p-tau181 and 5122 for NfL and GFAP; median age was 52.3 years and 49.7% were women. Between ages 40 and 80 years, expected NfL and GFAP concentrations increased by an average of 2.6% and 2.2% per year, respectively. Renal function, body-mass index, and sex had additional biomarker-specific effects. Application of the equations to clinical cohorts preserved distinct disease-associated profiles: NfL Z-scores were increased across disorders characterized by neuroaxonal injury, whereas p-tau181 and GFAP showed its greatest increase in Alzheimer disease. NfL cross-calibration equations showed excellent agreement between methods and matrices, with intraclass correlation coefficients greater than 0.90. InterpretationThis population-based multibiomarker framework enables blood biomarker concentrations to be interpreted relative to individuals with similar physiological characteristics. Publicly available equations, reference curves, and standardized Z-scores could improve individualized interpretation and comparability across biomarkers, laboratories, and clinical populations.

ImportanceRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a leading cause of hospitalization in high-risk young children during their second RSV season. Nirsevimab, a long-acting monoclonal antibody against RSV, is recommended for some children who are at increased risk for severe RSV and entering their second RSV season to prevent severe RSV disease. Because of limited eligibility criteria for second season nirsevimab, estimating its effectiveness is challenging. ObjectiveWe evaluated nirsevimab effectiveness against RSV-associated hospitalizations among young children eligible to receive nirsevimab during their second RSV season. DesignThis retrospective case control study with a test-negative design was conducted during the 2024-2025 and 2025-2026 RSV seasons. SettingEnrollment was in 26 hospitals across 24 U.S. states. ParticipantsChildren eligible for nirsevimab during their second RSV season (as recommended in August 2023) were included. Nirsevimab-eligible children 8-23 months old hospitalized with respiratory symptoms were enrolled. Case-patients tested positive for RSV; control-patients tested negative for RSV. ExposuresReceipt of nirsevimab before or during the childs second RSV season and at least 7 days before symptom onset. Outcomes and measuresRSV-associated hospitalization. Distinct circulating proteomic patterns associated with thoracic aortic diameter and stiffness: A proteome-wide study in the UK Biobank

Choi, A.; Hobbs, A. J.; Tsui, J.; Hingorani, A. D.; Paliwal, N.

BackgroundThoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA) is characterized by abnormal dilation and functional remodelling of the thoracic aorta. Current detection and surveillance rely largely on imaging, limiting population-wide risk stratification. Circulating proteins may provide biological insight into aortic remodelling and support future biomarker development. We aimed to identify circulating proteomic patterns associated with imaging-derived thoracic aortic diameter and aortic stiffness in a large population-based cohort. MethodsA proteome-wide association study was conducted in 6773 UK Biobank participants with available plasma proteomic and cardiac imaging data. A combined thoracic aortic diameter was derived from taking the maximum of standardized ascending and descending measurements, while stiffness was derived using a pressure-free index of aortic wall properties. Associations with 2920 circulating proteins were evaluated using multivariable linear regression with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the complete ranked protein list across Hallmark, curated, and Gene Ontology Biological Process collections. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed as a secondary analysis to explore shared and divergent variation between the two phenotypes. ResultsIn age- and sex-adjusted analyses, 358 proteins were associated with aortic diameter and 60 with stiffness after FDR correction. Sequential adjustment materially altered these associations; in the primary fully adjusted models, 417 proteins were associated with diameter and six with stiffness. Functional enrichment analyses highlighted complement/coagulation, extracellular matrix organization, and contractile pathways associated with aortic diameter. In contrast, although only a few individual proteins were associated with stiffness, rank-based pathway analysis across the full proteome identified a distinct enrichment pattern involving cytokine signalling, protein processing, and intracellular transport. Aortic diameter and stiffness were only modestly correlated (r = 0.21). Secondary PCA showed that the first principal component (PC1) largely overlapped with the diameter-associated profile, with 310 of 370 PC1-associated proteins also associated with diameter. Four proteins (REN, BCAN, GGH and THY1) were associated with both diameter and stiffness in addition to being associated with PC1. ConclusionCirculating proteomic profiling identified markedly different and minimally overlapping molecular patterns associated with thoracic aortic diameter and stiffness. Structural dilation was primarily linked to complement/coagulation, extracellular matrix, and contractile pathways, whereas stiffness reflected a smaller, more distinct aspect of aortic remodelling involving cytokine signalling and cellular transport processes. The four shared proteins across both phenotypes (REN, BCAN, GGH and THY1) represent candidates for further biomarker and causal investigation.
