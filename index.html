Variantscape: Retrospective Study of Large Language Model-Based Extraction of Variant-Treatment-Cancer Associations from Published Oncology Abstracts

Wosny, M.; Blindu, A. S.; Boesch, M.; Peres, T.; Niederhauser, T.; Fruh, M.; Rothermundt, C.; Hastings, J.

ObjectivesPrecision oncology relies on accurate interpretation of tumour-detected gene variants, to guide personalised treatment decisions. However, interpretation of variants in context requires extensive information that is often buried within unstructured biomedical literature and obscured by inconsistent nomenclature, making manual retrieval labour-intensive and prone to omissions. In this study, our aim was to evaluate the potential of large language models to extract variant-treatment-cancer associations at scale from published abstracts. DesignWe developed Variantscape, a large-scale, automated pipeline and open-access web tool. It integrates traditional natural language processing methods with state-of-the-art large language models to extract, standardise, and analyse co-associations between genetic variants, cancer types, and therapeutic interventions from published biomedical abstracts. ResultsFrom over 3.7 million abstracts published between January 2014 and October 2026, 886,195 articles containing gene names were eligible for extraction. Among these, 37,895 (4.3%) mentioned a variant, cancer type, and therapeutic agent together, covering 11,985 unique variants across 197 cancer types and 547 therapeutic agents. LLM verification against the abstract text confirmed at least one association in 35,224 papers, giving a literature-derived graph of 10,571 nodes and 44,644 verified edges. Canonical alterations in well-studied cancers (e.g., BRAF V600E in melanoma) were strongly linked to established treatments, while several rare variants also emerged with high-confidence literature support. ConclusionsBy applying large language models to biomedical literature, Variantscape enables scalable, context-aware extraction of trilateral variant-treatment-cancer relationships. This approach supports early evidence synthesis/hypothesis generation, highlights underrecognized or rare associations for MTB preparation, and offers a practical resource for accelerating discovery and supporting precision oncology research and translation. FundingThis study was funded by the School of Medicine at the University of St. Gallen in Switzerland under grant number 2300380. Article SummaryO_ST_ABSStrengths and limitations of this studyC_ST_ABS[bullet] Variantscape combines traditional natural language processing with large language models to enable scalable, automated extraction of variant-cancer-treatment relationships from over 3.7 million biomedical abstracts, substantially reducing the manual search burden inherent to existing molecular tumour board workflows. [bullet]The pipeline applies large language model-based inference to standardise inconsistent genetic nomenclature across the literature, improving the comparability and reliability of extracted associations that would otherwise be obscured by terminological variation. [bullet]Variantscape is designed as a continuously updatable, open-access resource, meaning it can incorporate newly published evidence without requiring repeated manual intervention. [bullet]The pipeline relies solely on abstract-level text rather than full-text articles, which may limit the depth and granularity of extracted associations, as key methodological details, variant context, and nuanced clinical findings are frequently reported only within the body of published papers. [bullet]Automated extraction using large language models introduces a risk of misattribution of variant-cancer-treatment relationships, and without systematic manual validation of outputs, the precision of identified associations at scale remains difficult to fully characterise.

Comprehensive real-world utilization, treatment trajectories, and outcomes of immune checkpoint inhibitors across cancer types: the multicenter FAIR-ICI cohort

Ha, L. T.; Ver Donck, F.; Bassez, I.; Oeste, C. L.; Vulsteke, C.; Delombaerde, D.; Hens, D.; Prenen, H.; Croes, L.; De Meulenaere, A.; Debruyne, P. R.; Verhaert, M.; Aspeslagh, S.; Brusselaers, N.; Verbiest, A.

BackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer treatment, yet evidence on their utilization, treatment trajectories, and effectiveness in routine clinical practice remains limited. We characterized real-world ICI use and outcomes across cancer types using the multicenter FAIR-ICI cohort and explored differences between real-world and pivotal trial outcomes. MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults receiving ICIs across four Belgian hospitals. Structured and unstructured electronic health record data were harmonized using the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership Common Data Model (OMOP-CDM) and natural language processing. We evaluated overall survival (OS), time on treatment (ToT), time to next treatment (TTNT), ICI discontinuation (>12-week treatment gap without re-initiation), and post-discontinuation survival. OS was descriptively compared with pivotal trials, and prognostic factors were assessed using multivariable Cox regression. An interactive public dashboard was developed to enable exploration of cohort characteristics, treatment patterns, and outcomes. ResultsAmong 3,231 ICI-treated patients, 2,514 (77.8%) had metastatic disease. Among metastatic patients, 568 (22.6%) discontinued ICI without subsequent systemic therapy. Median post-discontinuation OS was 21.4 months (95% CI 16.5-37.2), with 49.1% alive at 24 months. ToT and TTNT were generally shorter than OS, particularly in melanoma (median OS 78 months; ToT 7 months; TTNT 26 months). Real-world survival was frequently less favorable than in pivotal trials. ECOG [&ge;]2 was associated with poorer OS than ECOG 0-1 (adjusted HR 2.18, 95% CI 1.82-2.60). In NSCLC patients receiving pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy, survival with ECOG 0-1 more closely approximated trial outcomes, whereas survival with ECOG [&ge;]2 was substantially poorer. ConclusionsReal-world ICI treatment trajectories extend beyond active treatment, with some patients experiencing prolonged survival after discontinuation. Differences between real-world and trial outcomes highlight the importance of patient characteristics, particularly performance status, when interpreting trial evidence in routine care. FAIR-ICI provides a scalable framework for evaluating real-world immunotherapy effectiveness, treatment trajectories, and future investigations of treatment-related toxicity. Identifying interstitial lung disease with large language models on clinical notes and claims

Larghero, G.; Schneeweiss, S.; Gu, B.; Anand, P.; Lin, J. K.; Yang, J.

BackgroundInterstitial lung disease (ILD) is a potentially fatal toxicity of immune check-point inhibitors and antibody-drug conjugates. Drug-safety studies in healthcare databases identify ILD with claims-based algorithms that trade positive predictive value (PPV) against sensitivity. We assessed whether large language models (LLMs) improve on these algorithms, with and without electronic health record (EHR) notes, and whether these gains translate into measurable epidemiological benefits. MethodsIn 309 oncology patients with ILD diagnosis codes at Mass General Brigham, adjudicated by two pulmonologists, we compared 12 open-access LLMs with 20 published claims-based algorithms. LLMs received claims, EHR notes, or both, without task-specific training. Measures were PPV and yield (proportion classified positive). We projected the attenuation of a drug-induced ILD odds ratio under non-differential outcome misclassification. FindingsLLMs extended the PPV-yield frontier of the published algorithms with all three inputs. Across the 36 model-input evaluations, LLM PPV exceeded that of the best algorithm without specialist-coding requirements (Algorithm 4) by 14.3 percentage points on average. With claims alone, Qwen3-8B achieved 89.8% PPV (95% CI 78.2-95.6) at 15.9% yield, versus 86.7% at 4.9% for the highest-PPV algorithm. With notes alone, Qwen3-32B achieved 78.9% PPV at 49.2% yield, versus 64.6% at 51.1% for Algorithm 4. With combined claims and notes, Qwen3-14B achieved 100.0% PPV at 24.3% yield. Under a simulated drug-safety scenario with a true odds ratio of 3.0, the projected observed odds ratio was 2.67 with combined inputs versus 1.07 with Algorithm 4, corresponding to a 46-fold difference in the estimated sample size required for 80% power (591 versus 27,200 patients). InterpretationZero-shot LLMs offer a higher-PPV alternative to rule-based claims pheno-typing for ILD, on all three input types. Because the claims-only configuration uses the same claims data as existing algorithms, it could be deployed in administrative databases without linked notes, and may recover drug-safety signal otherwise lost to outcome misclassification. FundingNational Institute on Aging, National Library of Medicine, PCORI, and the National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases. Research in contextO_ST_ABSEvidence before this studyC_ST_ABSWe searched PubMed up to August 2026 for studies on claims or electronic health record (EHR)-based identification of interstitial lung disease and on machine-learning or large language model (LLM) phenotyping of clinical conditions. Search terms included "interstitial lung disease or pneumonitis", "administrative claims", "computational phenotyping", "rule-based algorithms", "machine learning", and "large language models". We also screened the reference lists of relevant articles. The literature search suggested that identifying ILD in longitudinal claims data is unreliable because the relevant diagnosis codes are nonspecific, and validated rule-based algorithms face a structural tradeoff between PPV and coverage. Supervised machine-learning classifiers have outperformed rule-based ILD identification on electronic health record data, and zero-shot LLMs have already shown the ability to phenotype chronic conditions from structured codes. No study, however, had compared a zero-shot LLM head-to-head against validated, published claims-based algorithms for a specific, high-stakes drug-safety outcome on a single fixed cohort, isolated the contribution of the input substrate from that of the model, or quantified the downstream consequence for pharmacoepidemiologic effect estimation. Added value of this studyOn a single, fixed, expert-adjudicated cohort, the evaluated LLMs achieved higher positive predictive value (PPV) than all 20 published claims-based algorithms at comparable yield (the proportion of patients classified as ILD-positive), whether the models received structured claims, clinical notes, or both. Across the 36 model-input evaluations, PPV exceeded that of the most inclusive published rule by 19.7 percentage points on average, and did so in 33 of 36 evaluations. We show that clinical notes generally increased yield, while the resulting PPV-yield trade-off depended on the model and input combination. Restricting the input to a single EHR note type preserved the advantage over rule-based phenotyping in matched comparisons on the patients possessing that EHR note type. Most consequentially, this improvement reduces the sample size required to detect a hypothesised drug-ILD association from approximately 27,200 patients for the best existing algorithm for the task to 591 when using LLMs, and offer a deployable upgrade for the note-free administrative databases where rule-based algorithms are the current standard. Implications of all the available evidenceFor pharmacovigilance of treatment-related ILD, the limiting step is often not the size of the available claims database but the misclassification that erodes the very signal under surveillance. Replacing rule-based claims phenotyping algorithms with zero-shot LLM-based alternatives transforms a high-PPV, low-yield classifier into one that recovers many more patients at the same PPV, converting a classifier that can render drug-safety associations statistically invisible into one that makes them detectable in cohorts of feasible size. Pending external validation, this offers a practical route to more sensitive post-marketing surveillance of the pulmonary toxicity of immune checkpoint inhibitors and antibody-drug conjugates. More broadly, we hypothesise that this advantage is not specific to ILD: zero-shot LLM phenotyping may recover drug-safety signal for other outcomes that diagnosis codes capture poorly but that are well documented in clinical text, a possibility that warrants dedicated evaluation.

Enhancing deep sleep with auditory stimulation to boost cognitive training responsiveness in people with Parkinson's disease and mild cognitive impairment: Rationale and design of the double-blind randomized controlled trial PD-CogT-Sleep

Ophey, A.; Kleinsorge, M. T.; Wallimann, N. A.; Maric, A.; Loosli, S. V.; Mollenhauer, B.; Balint, B.; Huber, R.; Baumann, C. R.; Kalbe, E.; Schreiner, S. J.

BackgroundCognitive training (CogT) is an established non-pharmacological intervention for people with Parkinsons disease (PD) and mild cognitive impairment (PD-MCI). However, overall effects on cognitive outcomes are small to moderate and interindividually highly heterogeneous. Sleep -especially deep sleep-could be an important determinant of CogT responsiveness in PD, and may have beneficial effects on PD pathophysiology. Therefore, the enhancement of deep sleep by phase-targeted auditory stimulation (PTAS) could strengthen the effects of CogT on cognitive symptoms and counteract disease progression. MethodsPD-CogT-Sleep is a monocentric, partly decentralized, double-blind, randomized sham-controlled trial that combines two remotely delivered non-pharmacological interventions, to assess the additive effect of PTAS on digital CogT responsiveness in people with PD-MCI. The primary outcome is executive functions; Secondary and exploratory outcomes include further cognitive functions, participant-reported outcome measures, blood-based biomarkers, and high-density sleep electroencephalography markers. Outcomes are assessed at baseline, five weeks post-allocation and at 3-month follow-up. We aim to consecutively recruit N=50 participants, who will be randomized 1:1 to receive CogT+PTAS or CogT+Sham. DiscussionThe PD-CogT-Sleep study integrates wearable and digital technologies to systematically combine a novel, non-invasive, sleep-enhancing intervention (PTAS) with established CogT to improve cognition and evaluate PTAS disease-modifying potential through its effects on blood-based biomarkers in PD-MCI. The expected results will foster our understanding of the interplay of sleep, cognition, and neurodegeneration, and may guide further development of safe, feasible, scalable, patient-centered, technology-based non-pharmacological interventions counteracting cognitive decline in neurodegenerative diseases. Alzheimers disease and psychiatric polygenic risk are disease-specific and independent of plasma biomarkers in a real-world young-onset dementia cohort.

Bhalala, O. G.; Longfei, W.; Gerring, Z. F.; Han, J.; Kang, M. J.; Trounson, B. O.; Loi, S. M.; Velakoulis, D.; Eratne, D.; Bahlo, M.; The MiND Study Group,

Background: Young onset dementia is frequently misdiagnosed, particularly where neurodegenerative and primary psychiatric disorders (PPD) overlap. Plasma biomarkers measure current pathology, whereas polygenic risk scores (PRS) capture lifelong genetic risk. Whether PRS derived from late-onset and population samples transfer to young-onset presentations, and how they relate to plasma biomarkers, is unknown. Methods: We studied 687 participants of European ancestry from the Markers in Neuropsychiatric Disorders (MiND) Study, a prospective young-onset neuropsychiatry cohort: 195 healthy controls (HC), 283 PPD, 55 Alzheimers disease (AD) and 154 non-AD neurodegenerative disease (oNDG). PRS for AD, a cross-disorder psychiatric factor (PSY), frontotemporal dementia, Lewy body dementia and Parkinsons disease were computed with PRS CS, excluding the APOE locus. Plasma NfL, GFAP and p-tau217 were available for 332 participants. Models were adjusted for age, sex and APOE e4 dose. Results: Relative to HC, the AD PRS was associated with AD (OR 1.69 per SD, 95% CI 1.17-2.45) but not PPD (0.95, 0.77-1.16). The PSY PRS showed the reciprocal pattern (PPD 1.29, 1.05-1.58; AD 1.00, 0.72-1.40). No PRS was associated with any plasma protein. In distinguishing PPD from oNDG, NfL was the strongest discriminator (OR 2.15, 1.62-2.83). The AD PRS was also associated with oNDG (1.38, 1.11-1.71), without improving discrimination (AUC 0.764 for NfL alone versus 0.773 combined). Conclusion: In young onset presentations, AD and psychiatric polygenic risk are disease-specific and independent of plasma biomarkers of neurodegeneration. Their combined diagnostic value requires evaluation in larger, longitudinal pre-clinical cohorts.

Which Attention Helps? A Slide-Level Ablation of Attention-Augmented CNNs for Serous Ovarian Carcinoma Grading

Rani, A.; Mishra, S.

BackgroundDistinguishing high-grade (HGSC) from low-grade serous carcinoma (LGSC) is clinically consequential yet difficult, as the subtypes share a serous morphology but differ in prognosis and treatment. Prior deep-learning studies report near-perfect accuracy, but often under patch-level evaluation that leaks slide information between training and testing. MethodsWe present a leakage-controlled, slide-level ablation of attention-augmented CNNs for this binary task on the UBC-OCEAN cohort (259 whole-slide images; 217 HGSC, 42 LGSC). Under an identical protocol with five-fold slide-grouped cross-validation, Macenko stain normalisation, and slide-level pooling, we compared a from-scratch backbone with no attention, Squeeze-and-Excitation, channel-only CBAM and full CBAM, a lightweight CBAM variant, and an ImageNet-pretrained MobileNet with a differential-attention module. A pathology foundation model (Phikon) with attention-based multiple-instance aggregation was evaluated as a modern benchmark. Results are reported across three training seeds, with decision thresholds selected within folds, and compared using DeLong and McNemar tests with Holm correction and bootstrap confidence intervals. ResultsThe pretrained model performed best (AUC 0.862 {+/-} 0.005, MCC 0.460) and was the most stable across seeds. It significantly outranked both the Squeeze-and-Excitation model (DeLong, Holm p = 0.002) and the no-attention baseline (p = 0.019). Full CBAM also outranked Squeeze-and-Excitation (p = 0.010), but adding the spatial-attention branch to channel attention produced no detectable change (0.824 to 0.822; p = 0.50), and the lightweight model did not match the deep one once training variance was accounted for. The foundation-model benchmark reached AUC 0.834 {+/-} 0.018, below the pretrained MobileNet. A colour-only classifier reached AUC 0.66, indicating part of the separability is attributable to stain and colour rather than morphology. ConclusionUnder leakage-free evaluation, performance is more modest than commonly reported. Transfer learning and the avoidance of a weak channel-only configuration produced the clearest gains, while finer attention design, network size and a pathology-specific foundation model produced no detectable improvement. With only 42 LGSC slides the task remains underpowered for finer distinctions, and most architectural differences could not be resolved.
