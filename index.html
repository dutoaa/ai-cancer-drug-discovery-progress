Privacy-Preserving Large Language Model Deployment for Oncology Registry Abstraction: Structure-Aware Evaluation in a Real-World Clinical Setting

Moldovan, M.; Enikeev, R.; Chu, M.; Amalraj, A.; Koli, P. P.; Abdul, S. S.; Sivaraj, H.; Iqbal, U.; Toh, C. K.

Objectives Structuring oncology clinical notes into registry-grade variables is essential for research and care but remains labour-intensive and error-prone. This study developed and evaluated a privacy-preserving large language model pipeline for oncology registry abstraction in a real-world clinical setting.<br> Methods An open-source Meta Llama 3.3 70B-based pipeline, prompted without task-specific fine-tuning, was deployed to extract over 50 variables from 6,700 oncology notes at a cancer centre in Singapore. Data were de-identified locally using a Hide-In-Plain-Sight approach, ensuring no identifiable data left hospital infrastructure. Performance was assessed on 200 randomly sampled notes with adjudicated ground truth, using a structure-aware framework that classified outputs as correct, missing, spurious, or incorrect.<br> Results F1 scores were high across variables, including diagnosis (97.2%), histology (95.8%), stage (92.6%), biomarkers (91.4%), and treatments (88.1%). Preliminary transferability testing on 50 external notes showed strong performance for core diagnostic variables, with reduced performance for list-valued variables.<br> Discussion The structure-aware evaluation approach separated omissions from fabrications, providing more clinically actionable insight than aggregate metrics alone.<br> Conclusion Privacy-preserving large language models achieved high extraction accuracy under a clinician-verified reference standard, supporting their feasibility for oncology registry abstraction in real-world clinical settings. Keywords: Large language models; Oncology registry abstraction; Natural language processing; Privacy-preserving AI; Clinical data extraction<br> What is already known on this topic Structuring oncology clinical notes into registry-grade variables is essential for cancer research and care but remains labour-intensive, costly, and inconsistent from one reviewer to the next when performed manually. Large language models offer potential to automate this task, but their use in real-world registries has been limited by concerns over patient data privacy, reliance on task-specific fine-tuning, and evaluation methods that obscure the difference between missing and fabricated information.<br> What this study adds This study shows that an open-source, privacy-preserving large language model pipeline, deployed entirely within hospital infrastructure without task-specific fine-tuning, can extract over 50 registry variables from oncology notes with high accuracy (F1 scores 88-97% across key variables), validated against ground truth checked by two clinicians. Using a structure-aware evaluation framework, we demonstrate that errors can be meaningfully separated into missing, spurious, and incorrect categories, offering clearer insight into model behaviour than aggregate accuracy metrics alone, and show preliminary evidence of transferability to notes from an external setting.<br> How this study might affect research, practice or policy These findings support the feasibility of deploying privacy-preserving, locally hosted large language models for oncology registry abstraction without requiring identifiable data to leave hospital systems or extensive model customisation. The structure-aware evaluation framework is also shared as a reusable template, enabling other institutions to validate similar AI extraction tools in a consistent, clinically meaningful way before wider implementation.</br>

Clinical referral text overrides MRI evidence in multimodal large language models: glioma versus brain abscess

Beste, N. C.; Bauer, F.; Lopez, N.; Schoemig, T.; Schoenfeld, M.; Zopfs, D.; Goertz, L.; Persigehl, T.; Kabbasch, C.; Schlamann, M.; Maintz, D.; Gertz, R. J.; Weitzer, N.; Kottlors, J.

ObjectivesTo determine how strongly clinical referral text, rather than image content, drives the decisions of multimodal large language models (LLMs) differentiating glioma from brain abscess on MRI. Materials and methodsThis retrospective single-center study included 60 contrast-enhanced T1-weighted brain MRI examinations (30 gliomas, 30 abscesses; reference-standard diagnosis). One axial slice per case was paired with five referral texts (explicitly inflammatory to explicitly tumor-suggestive) under three prompt instructions and submitted 20 times each to GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 5, Gemini 3.5 Flash, and Qwen 3.6 (72,000 answers). The primary outcome was the text-driven span (difference in tumor-call frequency between explicit inflammatory and explicit tumor text). Two blinded radiologists classified the images without text; sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were compared descriptively. ResultsPatients (29 women) had a mean age of 55.8 {+/-} 16.4 years. With neutral text, models answered tumor in 45-67% of queries (specificity, 0.38-0.78), versus 52% and 60% for radiologists (specificity, 0.77 and 0.63). The text-driven span was 0.79-1.00 (p < 0.001 for all models), whereas confirmed gliomas and abscesses differed by only 0.01-0.10 in three models and 0.25 in Gemini 3.5 Flash. Subtle wording produced at least 83% of the span in three models. An instruction to ignore the text reduced the span to 0.48-0.70 in three models and to 0.04 in Gemini 3.5 Flash, partly through reversion to a tumor default. Model accuracy was 0.52-0.73 versus 0.78 and 0.73 for radiologists. ConclusionMultimodal LLMs deferred to referral text over imaging evidence in differentiating glioma from brain abscess, and prompt instructions did not reliably correct this. Key PointsO_ST_ABSQuestionC_ST_ABSMultimodal large language models receive images together with clinical referral text, but how strongly the text rather than the image determines their diagnosis is unknown. FindingsChanging only the referral text largely reversed the glioma-versus-abscess decision for identical MRI images in four models; instructions to ignore the text reduced this inconsistently. Clinical relevanceBefore clinical use, multimodal large language models require model- and task-specific testing of their susceptibility to referral text, because instructing them to prioritize the image is not a dependable safeguard against text-driven misdiagnosis.

AI-Assisted Interpretation of Neurologic Autoantibody Cell-Based Assays

Qi, Z.; Cao, Z.; Tan, H.; Tang, B.; Shi, S. X.; Jia, R.; Yao, Y.; Chen, J.; Zhang, Z.; Wan, J.; Zheng, P.

Neurologic autoantibody cell-based assay images are difficult to interpret when fluorescence is weak, focal or dominated by background signal. We developed a visual artificial intelligence overlay that transfers detected cell boundaries from the structural channel to the antibody-staining image and evaluated standalone model concordance and its association with reader classifications. The analysis used 2 763 records for model development and 499 held-out records spanning 14 antibody targets. Four readers assessed the held-out records first without assistance and then with the boundary overlay and model category available. The selected model agreed with the reader-derived three-class reference in 85.2% of records, but recall was lowest for weak-positive or equivocal records. Reader agreement increased in the assisted session, and almost all revised ratings moved towards the model category. The combined display may support consistent visual review while also creating a risk of model-directed anchoring; its role as a second-read aid requires independent validation.
