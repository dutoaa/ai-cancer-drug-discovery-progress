Performance of a foundation model to generate thousands of predicted outcomes across multiple use cases

Mischiati, M.; Vattis, K.; Gliklich, R.; Leavy, M. B.; Boussios, C.; Bandaria,, J.

BackgroundPredictive artificial intelligence (AI) models hold great promise for medical care. While many types of models have proliferated across healthcare and the life sciences, systematic, rapid generation of multi-condition predictions of diagnoses, future medical events and future clinical outcomes from a single foundation-model architecture remains rare. PhenOM(R) is a generative AI foundation model trained and validated on longitudinal medical-event data from more than 400 million patient records in the OM1 Real World Data Cloud (RWDC) and designed to complete predictive tasks for a broad array of medical diagnoses, events and clinical outcomes (collectively outcomes) at varying time intervals, currently up to five (5) years. This paper describes the implementation of PhenOM to complete a vast number of predictive tasks across clinical domains and outcomes. MethodsPhenOM was evaluated across four outcome categories (Future Risk, Screening, Care Opportunity, and Treatment Effect) in multiple domains spanning chronic disease, acute medical events, healthcare utilization, and rare and frequently undiagnosed conditions. Performance was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Outcomes were defined within an eligible-population framework using standardized medical code sets (ICD-10, SNOMED, CPT, NDC, LOINC) unless outcomes could not be specified with an existing code. All benchmarks reported were derived from data excluded from training. ResultsAs of this publication, the single PhenOM model has been used to complete and validate thousands of predictive tasks covering more than 1,770 outcomes spanning most organ systems and clinical specialties, with new predictive tasks being validated each day. Overall, 80% of predictive tasks achieved AUROC of at least 0.70 and 30% achieved AUROC of at least 0.80. We present a sample of 177 of these tasks as examples that show the breadth of tasks the model can complete. Performance varied systematically by outcome and clinical domain. ConclusionsPhenOM demonstrates the ability to complete thousands of predictive tasks across clinical domains and outcomes at the individual patient level, without additional training. Outcomes include screening for undiagnosed conditions, predicting future conditions, medical events, effects of treatments, and assessing care opportunities. These can be ascertained at the individual patient and population levels. These findings support applications in individual and population health management, risk management, drug development, and real-world evidence generation, and establish a transparent, reproducible performance reference for a large-scale real-world predictive AI platform.

MedMLX: On-Device Medical Imaging AI for Apple Silicon

Sandoval, A.; Sandoval, J.; Saleem, A.

BackgroundMedical imaging research increasingly combines specialized artificial intelligence models for segmentation, detection, image synthesis and report generation. For three-dimensional CT and MRI, these workflows typically run on NVIDIA GPUs with substantial memory. On-device AI on widely available Apple Silicon computers could broaden access to these models for clinical researchers. These computers share a large memory pool between the CPU and GPU, which suits workflows that combine several models. However, few established imaging models run natively on Apple Silicon. Adapting a model to new hardware also requires verifying that its results match the original software. MethodsWe developed MedMLX, a collection of 21 established medical imaging models rewritten for Apple Silicon in MLX, Apples machine learning framework. The collection includes nnU-Net, TotalSegmentator, MedSAM2 and SAM 3. The models segment organs and tumors, detect lung nodules, generate synthetic images and draft radiology reports from CT, MRI, PET/CT, radiographs and endoscopic images. Using public cases from 11 imaging datasets, we compared the models results with those of the original software run on an Intel CPU and an NVIDIA GPU. Agreement required a Dice coefficient of at least 0.99 for every segmentation label and equivalent criteria for other outputs. We measured memory use and processing time on an Apple M1 Max computer with 64 GB of memory. ResultsMedMLX agreed with the original software in 211 of 212 comparisons. The single exception was a brain MRI segmentation. On this case, the original software itself gave different results on the two devices. We illustrated sequential workflows, including anatomical segmentation of a synthetic brain MRI and lesion synthesis guided by organ segmentation. With a memory-saving setting, 19 of the 21 models used less than 16 GB of memory. This setting reduced memory use by a median of 49% and increased processing time by a median of 13%. ConclusionsMedMLX enables on-device AI with established medical imaging models on Apple Silicon computers rather than specialized NVIDIA hardware. The models agree closely with the original software, and most need less than 16 GB of memory.

PedDx: An End-to-End Deep Learning Framework for Pediatric Posterior Fossa Tumor Diagnosis with Agentic AI-Enabled Independent External Evaluation

Fathi Kazerooni, A.; Batal, F.; Vunnava, B.; Damaraju, E.; Tavallaii, A.; Gandhi, D.; Gottipati, A.; Zhu, P.; Chrysochoou, D.; Varshochi, S.; Adib Moradi, S.; Familiar, A. M.; Chiang, A.; Vaghani, Y.; Azemi, G.; Doig, M.; To, M.-S.; Luck, A.; Kim, M. C.; Khalili, N.; Khalili, N.; Jain, P.; Lee, C.; Hansford, J. R.; Di Ieva, A.; Storm, P. B.; Resnick, A. C.; Reddy, K.; Vossough, A.; Nabavizadeh, A.

Reliable clinical translation of diagnostic artificial intelligence (AI) requires evaluation beyond classification accuracy. We developed PedDx, a segmentation-free framework for pediatric posterior fossa tumor diagnosis using T2-weighted MRI, pretrained 3DINO representations, calibrated predictions, and confidence-based abstention. Evaluation included 492 discovery, 114 replication, 129 external validation, and 40 independently tested cases, alongside 236 out-of-distribution (OOD) cases and controlled imaging perturbations. Classification accuracy was 79.8% in replication and 81.4% in external validation, increasing to 90.5% and 88.7%, respectively, among non-abstained cases. OOD analyses revealed performance degradation and confidently incorrect predictions, while perturbation analyses identified acquisition and alignment vulnerabilities. An external institution independently executed the complete agentic workflow, successfully generating predictions and structured quality-control reports for all 40 cases, with classification accuracy of 92.5%, increasing to 97.3% among non-abstained cases. PedDx demonstrates how uncertainty assessment, failure-mode characterization, external validation, and independently reproducible, traceable reporting can collectively inform the reliability and limitations of diagnostic AI systems.

Longitudinal AI Models Predict Cancer Adverse Events

Fu, C.; Yu, J.; Shi, R.; Waters, M.; Pavlak, M.; Arfe, A.; Shah, S. P.; Battle, A.; kehl, K. L.; Bot, B. M.; US Cancer AI Alliance, ; Schultz, N.; Morris, Q. D.; Jee, J.

Clinical interventions for cancer treatment toxicities can reduce morbidity, mortality, and healthcare cost, if high risk patients can be identified. We developed SPARC, a transformer-based foundation model, using real-world, longitudinal, routinely collected health measurements, to predict adverse event risk. Pre-training and fine-tuning SPARC on 101,920,379 measurements from 224,047 patients with cancer led to state-of-the-art performance at predicting cancer-related adverse events on (i) held-out data from the development cohort, (ii) a prospective validation cohort from the same center (Npatients=23,126), (iii) multiple publicly available external datasets (Npatients=26,012), and (iv) external, multi-hospital cohorts (Npatients=562,620, 206,840). SPARC generalized to multiple external centers and identified patients most likely to benefit from an intervention (G-CSF). Pooled multi-institution, more than single-institution, fine-tuning improved generalizability. We release SPARC as a community resource. Our results highlight the utility of AI time series models in oncologic management.

Machine learning-assisted serum Raman spectroscopy for risk stratification in men with suspected prostate cancer: a retrospective proof-of-concept study

Milligan, K.; Smith, I.; Flugge, A.; Fakhrua Aldeen, N.; Shine, B.; Muldoon, C.

BackgroundSerum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) remains the principal laboratory investigation for men with suspected prostate cancer but has limited specificity, particularly in patients with PSA concentrations between 4 and 10 ng/mL. Additional blood-based tests capable of improving risk stratification before imaging or biopsy could reduce unnecessary investigations while maintaining diagnostic sensitivity. Raman spectroscopy provides a biochemical fingerprint of biological samples and, when combined with machine-learning methods, may offer a novel approach to serum-based cancer detection. MethodsArchived serum samples obtained from men with histologically confirmed prostate cancer and symptomatic control subjects were analysed using dried-serum Raman spectroscopy. Raman spectra were incorporated into supervised machine-learning classification models to generate predicted probabilities of prostate cancer. Diagnostic performance was evaluated in the complete cohort and separately in patients with PSA concentrations between 4 and 10 ng/mL. ResultsThe study included 119 men with prostate cancer and 60 symptomatic controls. Predicted probabilities of prostate cancer were significantly higher in cancer cases than controls. The classifier demonstrated moderate discrimination in the complete cohort (AUC approximately 0.77) and maintained similar performance in patients with PSA concentrations between 4 and 10 ng/mL (54 prostate cancer and 41 symptomatic controls, AUC approximately 0.78). ConclusionsMachine learning-assisted analysis of dried-serum Raman spectra shows promise as an adjunct to PSA testing. The maintenance of diagnostic performance within the PSA grey zone suggests a potential role as a second-line risk-stratification test before MRI or biopsy. Prospective validation within contemporary diagnostic pathways is now required.

Multiscale Spatial Characterization of Glioma MRI with MUSE and Radiomics

Miola, M.; Cammarasana, S.

PurposeCharacterizing glioma heterogeneity requires measurements that retain the physical scale and orientation of intratumoral intensity dependence. We evaluate the original 3D variographic field produced by the MUSE tool (Modeling Uncertainty as a Support for the Environment) alongside directional radiomics and independent image-based molecular classification. MethodsWe analyzed postcontrast T1-weighted magnetic resonance images and enhancing-tumor segmentations from the public UCSF-PDGM collection. Verified MUSE outputs were available for 416 independent baseline patients, of whom 375 had paired directional gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) measurements. Each MUSE field retained 328 original experimental directional variograms with physical lag distances, semivariances, orientations, and voxel-pair support. Agreement was assessed at aligned offsets and through between-tumor comparisons. Patient-level permutation tests evaluated associations with isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) status on 2,508 native direction-lag coordinates. A standalone 3D residual convolutional neural network (CNN) provided a separate IDH classification benchmark. ResultsAt 9,371 geometrically aligned offsets, native MUSE semivariance and half GLCM contrast correlated strongly (Pearson r = 0.954). Across 31,599 eligible between-tumor comparisons, representation-level dissimilarities showed Spearman{rho} S = 0.862, with sensitivity to GLCM feature family and long-offset sampling support. In 364 grade-4 tumors, including 24 IDH-mutant cases, the original MUSE field was associated with IDH after conventional adjustment (global permutation p = 0.00050), but the association was attenuated after primary directional GLCM adjustment (p = 0.256). The independent CNN achieved an out-of-fold IDH ROC-AUC of 0.792 and PR-AUC of 0.414 across 416 patients. Native MUSE examples revealed scale- and direction-dependent spatial behavior, including distances beyond those sampled by the GLCM protocol. ConclusionVariograms by MUSE provide a physically indexed description of tumor spatial heterogeneity that can accompany radiomic analysis and CNN classification. The observed statistical overlap is substantial, and incremental clinical discrimination beyond directional GLCM features remains unestablished.

QUARTZ: Cross-Dataset Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Retinal Vasculometry

Welikala, R. A.; Fajtl, J.; Johnson, G.; Rahman, F.; Remagnino, P.; Freeman, E. E.; Kolko, M.; Chambers, R.; Bolter, L.; Anderson, J.; Olvera-Barrios, A.; Warwick, A.; Foster, P. J.; Khawaja, A. P.; Shakespeare, R.; Ramesh, M.; Egan, C.; Tufail, A.; Owen, C. G.; Rudnicka, A. R.; Barman, S.; UK Biobank Eye and Vision Consortium, ; EPIC-Norfolk Investigators, ; ARIAS Research Group,

Examination of retinal images provides a non-invasive means to assess the bodys microvascular system, with vessel morphology linked to ocular and systemic diseases. QUARTZ (QUantitative Analysis of Retinal vessel Topology and siZe) is an automated, artificial intelligence-enabled retinal vasculometry system designed for processing large-scale retinal image datasets, including over 4 million images from within the National Health Service, to facilitate epidemiological studies. The system is uniquely capable of extracting useful information from lower quality images, including those which are partially illuminated, enabling analysis of a greater proportion of images. To enhance cross-dataset generalisation of the system from an earlier dataset-tailored approach, this work leverages 26 annotated retinal image datasets spanning diverse demographics, diseases, camera types, and imaging protocols. Evaluation on the external EPIC-Norfolk dataset demonstrates robust performance with F1 scores of 0.8305, 0.8477, 0.8741, 0.9532, and 0.8677 for arteriole, venule, vessel, optic disc, and optic cup segmentation, respectively, and 0.9197 for image quality classification. The cross-dataset generalisation approach demonstrated increased performance for arteriole/venule segmentation and optic disc segmentation. However, improvements in performance were not observed for optic cup segmentation, or for image quality classification. An online tool is provided to support wider adoption and reproducible biomarker research across diverse populations and imaging settings. In addition, the new public CHASE-AV database is introduced for arteriole and venule segmentation.

Serum Raman spectroscopy as a blood-based triage test for bladder cancer: a retrospective proof-of-concept study

Milligan, K.; Smith, I.; Flugge, A.; Fakhrua Aldeen, N.; Shine, B.; Muldoon, C.

BackgroundBladder cancer remains one of the most common urological malignancies. Current diagnostic pathways rely heavily on cystoscopy, an invasive investigation with significant cost and resource implications. A rapid blood-based assay capable of improving patient stratification before cystoscopy could reduce unnecessary invasive investigations while maintaining diagnostic sensitivity. MethodsArchived serum samples from patients with histologically confirmed bladder cancer and symptomatic control subjects were analysed using dried-serum Raman spectroscopy. Raman spectra were incorporated into supervised machine-learning classification models to generate predicted probabilities of bladder cancer. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. ResultsThe study included 49 patients with bladder cancer and 97 symptomatic controls. Predicted probabilities of bladder cancer were substantially higher in cancer patients than in controls. The Raman classifier demonstrated excellent diagnostic discrimination (AUC approximately 0.90). Incorporation of age into the classification model produced little additional improvement (AUC approximately 0.91). ConclusionsMachine learning-assisted analysis of dried-serum Raman spectra demonstrates promising diagnostic performance for bladder cancer. Prospective evaluation is warranted to determine its role as a minimally invasive triage test within contemporary bladder cancer diagnostic pathways.
