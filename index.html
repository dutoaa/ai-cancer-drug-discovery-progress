Integrating Histopathology Foundation Models and PRAME Immunohistochemistry in Melanoma Diagnosis

Gellrich, F. F.; Kistner, A.; Guenther, C.; Hufnagel, C.; Hobelsberger, S.; Laske, J.; Beissert, S.; Aust, D.; Baretton, G.; Reidow, N.; Sergon, M.; Kather, J. N.; Ghaffari Laleh, N.

BackgroundThe histopathological diagnosis of challenging melanocytic lesions remains limited by substantial interobserver variability. Although computational pathology foundation models demonstrated promising diagnostic performance, their predictions are often difficult to interpret in terms of established criteria. ObjectiveWe evaluated a diagnostic framework combining foundation-model-based whole-slide image analysis, automated prediction of morphological features, and manually assessed PRAME immunohistochemistry. MethodsUsing the Virchow2 foundation model and STAMP framework, we analyzed a retrospective cohort of 1,000 melanocytic lesions undergoing PRAME immunohistochemistry. Models were trained on 800 cases and evaluated on a temporally held-out test cohort of 200 cases. ResultsThe slide-level malignancy classifier achieved a test AUROC of 0.970 (AUPRC 0.984). Separate models predicted established histopathological criteria with high discrimination, including ulceration (AUROC 0.991) and dermal mitoses (AUROC 0.966). In contrast, predicting PRAME expression from H&E images alone showed lower performance (AUROC 0.776-0.780 for PRAME scores 4 and 0). An integrated model combining the slide-level malignancy score, AI-predicted morphological features, and manual PRAME status achieved an AUROC of 0.972 (AUPRC 0.975), comparable to the standalone classifier. In multivariable analysis, PRAME score 4 versus 0 was strongly associated with malignancy (adjusted OR 35.04, 95% CI 17.39-74.62). ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate the feasibility of combining foundation-model-based histopathological analysis with established morphological criteria and PRAME immunohistochemistry. Although the integrated framework did not substantially improve overall discrimination, it provides a structured approach for incorporating complementary histopathological and immunohistochemical information into AI-assisted assessment of melanocytic lesions. Artificial intelligence (AI) computer programs can accurately analyze digitized tissue samples, but they often function as "black boxes," making it difficult for doctors to understand how the AI reached its conclusion. In this study, we tested an AI system on 1,000 skin samples. Instead of relying solely on the AI to provide a final diagnosis, we combined its analysis with specific microscopic features that doctors routinely look for, alongside the results of a special laboratory test called PRAME. We found that the AI system was highly accurate in identifying melanoma. While combining the AI with the specific tissue features and the PRAME test did not further increase the overall accuracy, it successfully aligned the computers output with established medical standards. Deep Learning of Fluorescence Lifetime Imaging Ophthalmoscopy for Type 2 Diabetes Classification

Kwon, S.; Lee, C. S.; Lee, A. Y.; Zhang, L.

PurposeTo evaluate whether fluorescence lifetime imaging ophthalmoscopy (FLIO) combined with deep learning can detect metabolic signatures for classification of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). DesignCross-sectional analysis of participants included in the AI-READI dataset (version 3) with FLIO imaging. Subjects1,783 participants from the AI-READI dataset (version 3) with HbA1c measurements and FLIO imaging scans: 425 normoglycemic, 569 prediabetic, and 789 diabetic. MethodsGlycemic groups were defined from self-reported diagnosis, glucose-lowering medication, and HbA1c. Center-of-mass mean lifetime ({tau}CMM) maps, corrected for background and system timing offset, were generated from the raw time-resolved FLIO scans. Group differences in central-window {tau}CMM were tested by one-way ANOVA and by age-adjusted analysis of covariance. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), ResNet-18, and XGBoost were trained on {tau}CMM maps for three-class (normal, prediabetic, diabetic) and two binary (normal vs. impaired; normal vs. diabetic) classification of T2DM and compared them with an age-only classifier and with models combining FLIO and age. Main Outcome MeasuresMacro-averaged area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, F1 score, and positive predictive value (PPV). ResultsGroup-averaged {tau}CMM was longer in prediabetic and diabetic than in normoglycemic participants in both the SSC (p < 0.001) and the LSC (p < 0.001), but only the SSC difference remained significant after age adjustment (p = 0.017). For 3-class classification, the CNN using FLIO achieved the highest AUROC (0.60 {+/-} 0.04), compared with 0.51 {+/-} 0.03 for an age-only classifier. ResNet-18 and XGBoost showed similar performance. Binary classification of normal vs. diabetic participants yielded AUROC 0.67 {+/-} 0.04 (CNN), 0.64 {+/-} 0.04 (ResNet-18), and 0.62 {+/-} 0.04 (XGBoost). All three FLIO models exceeded the age-only classifier in all T2DM classification tasks, whereas adding age to the FLIO models did not improve model performance. ConclusionsFLIO-derived {tau}CMM maps capture retinal signals associated with diabetes status that exceed what age alone provides, but yield modest classification performance with current artificial intelligence models. These findings highlight both the potential and the challenges of using FLIO for early metabolic screening and monitoring, informing future development of clinically applicable imaging biomarkers.

Population-based reference equations and Z-scores for blood biomarkers of neurodegenerative diseases

Lehmann, S.; Andriambelosoa, T.; Morchikh, M.; Mortamais, M.; Duchiron, M.; Gabelle, A.; Hirtz, C.; Ayrignac, X.; Busto, G.; Bennys, K.; Kab, S.; Helmer, C.; Zins, M.; Helmer, C.; Mura, T.

BackgroundBlood biomarkers are increasingly used to support the diagnosis and monitoring of neurodegenerative diseases. However, their interpretation is complicated by physiological determinants, including age, sex, body-mass index, and renal function, and by differences in absolute concentrations between analytical methods. We aimed to develop population-based reference equations allowing individualized interpretation of selected blood biomarkers blood biomarkers used in neurology. MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we analysed plasma samples from cognitively unimpaired participants selected from the French CONSTANCES and Three-City population-based cohorts. Generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape were used to model neurofilament light chain (NfL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), phosphorylated tau 181 (p-tau181), amyloid-{beta}40, amyloid-{beta}42, and their ratios according to age, sex, body-mass index, and renal function. The resulting equations provided individualized expected concentrations, percentiles, and Z-scores. Previously established disease-specific concentrations were converted into Z-score. Cross-calibration equations were developed for NfL measurements across analytical methods and sample matrices. FindingsThe final reference populations comprised 5123 participants for amyloid biomarkers and p-tau181 and 5122 for NfL and GFAP; median age was 52.3 years and 49.7% were women. Between ages 40 and 80 years, expected NfL and GFAP concentrations increased by an average of 2.6% and 2.2% per year, respectively. Renal function, body-mass index, and sex had additional biomarker-specific effects. Application of the equations to clinical cohorts preserved distinct disease-associated profiles: NfL Z-scores were increased across disorders characterized by neuroaxonal injury, whereas p-tau181 and GFAP showed its greatest increase in Alzheimer disease. NfL cross-calibration equations showed excellent agreement between methods and matrices, with intraclass correlation coefficients greater than 0.90. InterpretationThis population-based multibiomarker framework enables blood biomarker concentrations to be interpreted relative to individuals with similar physiological characteristics. Publicly available equations, reference curves, and standardized Z-scores could improve individualized interpretation and comparability across biomarkers, laboratories, and clinical populations.

Distinct circulating proteomic patterns associated with thoracic aortic diameter and stiffness: A proteome-wide study in the UK Biobank

Choi, A.; Hobbs, A. J.; Tsui, J.; Hingorani, A. D.; Paliwal, N.

BackgroundThoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA) is characterized by abnormal dilation and functional remodelling of the thoracic aorta. Current detection and surveillance rely largely on imaging, limiting population-wide risk stratification. Circulating proteins may provide biological insight into aortic remodelling and support future biomarker development. We aimed to identify circulating proteomic patterns associated with imaging-derived thoracic aortic diameter and aortic stiffness in a large population-based cohort. MethodsA proteome-wide association study was conducted in 6773 UK Biobank participants with available plasma proteomic and cardiac imaging data. A combined thoracic aortic diameter was derived from taking the maximum of standardized ascending and descending measurements, while stiffness was derived using a pressure-free index of aortic wall properties. Associations with 2920 circulating proteins were evaluated using multivariable linear regression with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the complete ranked protein list across Hallmark, curated, and Gene Ontology Biological Process collections. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed as a secondary analysis to explore shared and divergent variation between the two phenotypes. ResultsIn age- and sex-adjusted analyses, 358 proteins were associated with aortic diameter and 60 with stiffness after FDR correction. Sequential adjustment materially altered these associations; in the primary fully adjusted models, 417 proteins were associated with diameter and six with stiffness. Functional enrichment analyses highlighted complement/coagulation, extracellular matrix organization, and contractile pathways associated with aortic diameter. In contrast, although only a few individual proteins were associated with stiffness, rank-based pathway analysis across the full proteome identified a distinct enrichment pattern involving cytokine signalling, protein processing, and intracellular transport. Aortic diameter and stiffness were only modestly correlated (r = 0.21). Secondary PCA showed that the first principal component (PC1) largely overlapped with the diameter-associated profile, with 310 of 370 PC1-associated proteins also associated with diameter. Four proteins (REN, BCAN, GGH and THY1) were associated with both diameter and stiffness in addition to being associated with PC1. ConclusionCirculating proteomic profiling identified markedly different and minimally overlapping molecular patterns associated with thoracic aortic diameter and stiffness. Structural dilation was primarily linked to complement/coagulation, extracellular matrix, and contractile pathways, whereas stiffness reflected a smaller, more distinct aspect of aortic remodelling involving cytokine signalling and cellular transport processes. The four shared proteins across both phenotypes (REN, BCAN, GGH and THY1) represent candidates for further biomarker and causal investigation.

Wearable-based sleep and activity tracking detects Parkinson's disease and associated pathological hallmarks

Moustaklis, A.; Gerasimou, I.; Sotirakis, C.; Chairta, P.; Kurtis, M. M.; Del Campo, N.; Feige, T.; Schnalke, N.; Almarcha Menargues, M.-L.; Michailidou, K.; Christodoulou, K.; Fabbri, M.; Falkenburger, B. H.; Hadjileontiadis, L.; Hadjidimitriou, S.

Parkinsons disease (PD) is associated with sleep and motor function impairment, even in the early stages. Here, we investigated whether passive wearable-based sleep and activity tracking can detect PD and its associated pathological hallmarks, dopaminergic deficit and -synuclein pathology. Sleep and activity metrics (SAM) were derived from free-living wrist accelerometry data of a U.S. cohort and used to train a machine learning model (SAM-PD) to distinguish persons with PD (n = 133) from healthy controls (n = 26). The model achieved an AUROC of 0.93 and an AUPRC of 0.98, demonstrated excellent test-retest reliability, and generalized without retraining to an independent European cohort (n = 40; 50% PwPD) monitored with a different device (AUROC, 0.95; AUPRC, 0.95). REM sleep duration, walking cadence, and low-level acceleration features were most predictive. Applied to a U.S. at-risk cohort, SAM-PD performed comparably to the resource-intensive Movement Disorder Society (MDS) prodromal PD risk score in detecting dopaminergic deficit (AUROC, 0.86 vs. 0.80; AUPRC, 0.45 vs. 0.46; n = 121; 12.4% prevalence) and -synuclein pathology (AUROC, 0.76 for both; AUPRC 0.50 vs. 0.42; n = 114; 16.7% prevalence). A combination with the MDS score modestly improved detection. Findings support further evaluation of wearable monitoring for PD screening and biomarker enrichment in larger populations.

Wearable Sensors Predict Exertion Without Changes in Pulse Rate for Frail End-Stage Kidney Disease Patients

Deehring, M. E.; Lorenz, E. C.; Katzman, H. P.; Isac, R.; Sasangohar, F.; Dhala, A.; Yamagami, M.

End-Stage Kidney Disease (ESKD) patients require careful exertion monitoring during prehabilitation to ensure safety and efficacy. Traditional prehabilitation typically relies on heart rate to estimate physical exertion; however, this is problematic for ESKD patients due to their lower physical capacity and the high prevalence of beta-blocker prescriptions, which prevent typical heart rate responses during exercise. In this study, we investigated whether multi-modal wearable sensors could better predict the Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) in (N=13) ESKD patients and (N=13) healthy older adult controls. We collected continuous physiological data--including pulse rate, electrodermal activity, skin temperature, and accelerometer data--using a commercial smartwatch during prehabilitation sessions. Using individualized machine learning pipelines, we compared regression models built solely on pulse rate against comprehensive multi-feature models. We found distinct differences between the groups: baseline pulse-rate models performed substantially worse for the ESKD cohort, whereas they maintained predictive validity for controls. Conversely, multi-feature models significantly improved predictive performance for ESKD participants, achieving a Root Mean Square Error of 1.33. Feature selection confirmed that ESKD models relied heavily on physiological data beyond pulse rate to compensate for the lack of cardiovascular variability. These results demonstrate that heart rate alone is an insufficient biomarker for this population, and that remote exertion monitoring for ESKD patients requires distinct, multi-modal predictive algorithms rather than those designed for healthy adults.

Anatomy-Aware 3D GAN with Local Mask Refinement for Brain MRI Inpainting

Rahimi, A. F.; Rai, H. M.; Mushtaq, S. Y. A.

Brain tumor MRI inpainting aims to synthesize plausible healthy-appearing tissue in regions removed from pathological scans. We present an anatomy-aware three-dimensional generative adversarial network for Task 4 of the BraTS 2026 challenge. The method combines mask-aware gated convolutions, mirrored contralateral context, attention-modulated skip fusion, boundary-aware refinement, and a local masked-region refiner, each adapted from established components in the image synthesis and inpainting literature. The model is trained with a two-stage objective that first emphasizes voxel-wise and structural reconstruction and then applies low-weight adversarial fine-tuning. On a deterministic local held-out split, the proposed method substantially improves over a simple 3D AutoEncoder baseline but does not surpass the challenges Pix2Pix3D baseline. On the official BraTS 2026 Task 4 evaluation server, the submitted model obtained a mean SSIM of 0.658 and a mean PSNR of 14.81 dB over 219 evaluated cases, lower than the local held-out figures. We report these results transparently, analyze likely contributing factors, and identify the controlled ablations (removal of the mirrored-context channel and of the two-stage schedule) required to isolate the contribution of each architectural component.

Clinical relevance: This imaging research synthesizes healthy-appearing tissue within masked tumor regions; it does not demonstrate drug discovery or clinical benefit.

  • Combines mask-aware gated convolutions with mirrored contralateral context.
  • Uses attention-modulated skip fusion, boundary-aware refinement, and a local masked-region refiner.
  • Trains first for reconstruction, then adds low-weight adversarial fine-tuning.

Evidence signal: Official BraTS 2026 Task 4 evaluation across 219 cases yielded mean SSIM 0.658 and PSNR 14.81 dB, below local held-out results.

Watch item: Controlled ablations are still needed to isolate component contributions, and synthesized tissue should not be interpreted as verified healthy anatomy.
