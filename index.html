Statistical design of Path to Prevention platform trial in early neuronal alpha-synuclein disease

Wendelberger, B.; Allen-Savietta, C.; Crawford, A.; Brumm, M. C.; Choi, S. H.; Kopil, C. M.; Schneider, R. B.; Kuhl, M. M.; Lafontant, D.-E.; Ecklund, D.; Fabrizio, K.; Foroud, T.; Goldstein, F. S.; Kamp, C.; Kieburtz, K.; Larson, D. N.; Luthra, N.; Pavese, N.; Siderowf, A.; Stanley, C. E.; Tanner, C. M.; Tropea, T. F.; Willson, T.; Chowdhury, S.; Marek, K.; Simuni, T.; Coffey, C. S.

The Path to Prevention (P2P) platform trial is a first-in-class, phase 2A, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled learning study. Its design expedites the assessment of investigational therapies for the treatment of early-stage Neuronal -Synuclein Disease (NSD), a biological disease continuum that encompasses and often precedes clinically manifest Parkinsons Disease. P2P is embedded within the Parkinsons Precision Medicine Initiative (PPMI), a robust observational longitudinal cohort study on Parkinsons disease progression and a trial-facilitating enrollment framework. A regimen, comprised of an active investigational therapy and a matched placebo control, evaluates a therapeutic candidate within the P2P perpetual platform framework. Multiple regimens can be enrolled, enabling efficient testing of multiple therapeutics. A two-stage randomization scheme determines regimen sample size with 125 participants assigned to a regimens active therapy and a variable number to placebo control. The treated follow up period is a minimum of 96 weeks and a maximum of 144 weeks, with the last randomized participant followed for 96 weeks. As a pioneering interventional trial in early-stage NSD, P2P endpoint selection was critically informed by modeling and analysis of available PPMI data. In P2P, regimens will be evaluated for efficacy using two primary endpoints: 1) a biomarker outcome of Dopamine Transporter Single-Photon Emission Computed Tomography (DAT SPECT) imaging measured by the bilateral putamen Specific Binding Ratio (SBR) and 2) a clinical outcome measured by the Movement Disorder Society-Sponsored Revision of the Unified Parkinsons Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) Part III score. Each endpoint will be assessed separately using a longitudinal Bayesian mixed effects model, which compares the active treatment group to the shared placebo control group. The model uses regimen-specific controls at full weight and dynamically discounts controls from other regimens. Additionally, placebo control estimates are supplemented by non-randomized control data from the PPMI cohort. Clinical trial simulations, informed by longitudinal data from 275 P2P-eligible PPMI study participants (Age 60-81, 35% Female, 65% Male), were conducted to evaluate the design. Each regimen is well-powered to detect a 40% slowing in progression for both the clinical and biomarker primary endpoints when comparing active treatment to randomized control data. Supplementing the control estimation with non-randomized, P2P-eligible registry participant data increases power in the explored scenarios up to 4% for each endpoint. The embedded P2P platform trial is a powerful learning study that leverages statistical efficiencies from the platform trial design and established PPMI infrastructure and is poised to advance treatment development in early-stage NSD.

Genotypic infection endpoint analysis to understand efficacy and escape potential of the malaria monoclonal antibody CIS43LS

Schwabl, P.; Niangaly, A.; Nickols, W.; Callier, V.; Mateja, A.; Kayentao, K.; Ongoiba, A.; Skinner, J.; Preston, A. C.; Doumbo, S.; Doumtabe, D.; Amaya-Romero, J. E.; Kelley, K. K.; Li, S.; Laws, M.; Peterson, M. E.; Tran, T. M.; Murphy, S. C.; Traore, B.; Seder, R. A.; Crompton, P. D.; Potter, G. E.; Neafsey, D. E.

Early-phase trials of candidate malaria interventions in endemic areas often use "time-to-first" infection as a primary efficacy endpoint. This approach does not assess infection risk after the initial infection, and its censoring of trial participants after their first infection can introduce bias by removing more high-risk participants from control than treatment arms over time. Efficacy estimate precision may also decline as risk set size decreases. Here we assessed the utility of genotyping all infections in an intervention trial to determine the total infection incidence in each participant for the full follow-up period. Specifically, we genotyped all Plasmodium falciparum infections detected in a previously-reported phase 2 trial of the antimalarial monoclonal antibody CIS43LS in 330 Malian adults. We determined the molecular force of infection (molFOI; rate of new P. falciparum strains) for each participant, expressing efficacy as the percent molFOI reduction in CIS43LS versus placebo arms (EffmolFOI). Six-month EffmolFOI was 86.5% (95% CI: 80.1-90.8) and 70.7% (61.0-78.0) for the 40 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg doses, respectively. These estimates are similar to those from time-to-first infection analysis, but more precise when analyzing efficacy by trial-month and when stratifying by P. falciparum infection-status at enrollment. Participants infected at enrollment had lower EffmolFOI and acquired infections earlier in the trial, creating systematic differences in risk composition between arms over time. Genotyping showed no evidence for escape-polymorphisms in the CIS43LS epitope. These results demonstrate how genotypic endpoints can provide a more comprehensive understanding of candidate malaria interventions. One sentence summaryMalaria parasite genotyping and molecular force of infection inference improves standard malaria intervention efficacy analysis.

Polygenic overlap of physical activity with schizophrenia and Parkinson's disease

van der Walt, K.; Shadrin, A.; Tesfaye, M.; van der Meer, D.; Andreassen, O.; Rokicki, J.; Campbell, M.

Physical activity levels are increasingly investigated as candidate digital biomarkers across a range of psychiatric and neurological disorders. Physical activity is heritable, with associated genetic variants implicating genes expressed in the central nervous system. However, its shared genetic architecture with disorders such as schizophrenia and Parkinsons disease (PD) remains poorly understood. Here, we applied complementary genome-wide methods to investigate the genetic overlap between physical activity and these disorders. We used a genome-wide association study (GWAS) of objectively measured physical activity (n = 89,683), enhanced through multi-trait GWAS incorporating self-reported activity measures (n = 124,842-377,234). In cross-trait analysis with recent GWAS of schizophrenia (n = 53,386 cases, 77,258 controls) and PD (63,555 cases, 17,700 proxy cases, and 1,746,386 controls), we find that while genetic correlations are negligible (rg=-0.02-0.02, p>0.05), polygenic overlap was substantial, with the majority of trait-influencing variants shared between the three traits. This contrast reflects mixed effect directions, which we show through convergent results from causal mixture modelling, local genetic correlations, and conjunctional false discovery rate analysis. Genes shared between activity and schizophrenia were differentially expressed in the amygdala and several basal ganglia structures, and associated with a range of cognitive-affective traits. In contrast, genes shared between activity and PD were largely associated with neurodegenerative disorders and structural brain phenotypes. Together, these findings suggest that activity draws on genes involved in motor, cognitive, affective and neurodegenerative processes, providing a genetic basis for its alteration across diverse neuropsychiatric disorders.

Path to Prevention Platform Trial in Early-Stage Neuronal Alpha-Synuclein Disease: Study Design

Schneider, R. B.; Coffey, C. S.; Kopil, C. M.; Kuhl, M. M.; Allen-Savietta, C.; Brumm, M. C.; Choi, S. H.; Crawford, A.; Ecklund, D.; Fabrizio, K.; Foroud, T.; Goldstein, F. S.; Kamp, C.; Kieburtz, K.; Lafontant, D.-E.; Larson, D. N.; Luthra, N.; Pavese, N.; Siderowf, A.; Stanley, C. E.; Tropea, T. F.; Wendelberger, B.; Tanner, C. M.; Willson, T.; Chowdhury, S.; Marek, K.; Simuni, T.

The development of biologically targeted therapeutics (BTTs) that slow Parkinsons disease progression has been hampered by a reliance on testing interventions in clinically defined populations with already advanced pathobiology. Publications on the biological definition and classification of neuronal -synuclein disease (NSD), and the development of the NSD integrated staging system (NSD-ISS), have generated a framework for identifying individuals with NSD prior to the emergence of a clinically defined syndrome. Path to Prevention (P2P) is a phase 2A, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled platform trial designed to assess the safety and efficacy of interventions in an early-stage NSD population. The trial is embedded within the observational Parkinsons Precision Medicine Initiative (PPMI), a structure that enables recruitment of participants from the PPMI cohort, leverages existing operational infrastructure, and utilizes data from non-P2P enrolled PPMI participants in the Bayesian statistical modeling. P2P will enroll participants at least 60 years of age with NSD Stage 2B (abnormal -synuclein, dopaminergic dysfunction, and prodromal signs or symptoms without functional impairment) without a clinically defined diagnosis of PD or a related disorder. As a platform trial, P2P will enable the testing of multiple interventions under a Master Protocol at approximately 35 sites across North America and Europe. Interventions are selected from industry-submitted applications based on biological relevance of the targets, and on the adequacy of the safety data appropriate to the target population. Each treatment regimen will have 125 participants on active treatment and at least 125 shared placebo control participants. Participants will remain on treatment for a maximum of 144 weeks or until 96 weeks since the randomization of the last participant. P2P has two primary endpoints, 1) rate of change in mean putamen specific binding ratio from baseline through follow-up and, 2) rate of change in the MDS-UPDRS Part III motor examination from baseline through follow-up or initiation of dopaminergic treatment. A range of secondary and exploratory biomarker-focused endpoints will be assessed. The protocol was developed with input from multiple stakeholders, including people with early-stage NSD, biopharmaceutical industry leaders, and regulatory authorities. P2P is the first trial to test biologically targeted assets in biologically defined prodromal participants utilizing an efficient platform design, which will provide the field with tools to expand therapeutic development, and enable intervention at earlier stages of neurodegeneration. P2P is scheduled to begin enrollment in December 2026 and is committed to data sharing and broad dissemination of learnings from the study.

Suvorexant Inversely Affects Phosphorylated Tau in Human Cerebrospinal Fluid and Plasma

Pedersen, T. J.; Yu, J.; Li, A.; Ibanez, L.; Bubu, O. M.; Lucey, B. P.

Alzheimer disease (AD) is characterized by amyloid-{beta} plaques and neurofibrillary tangles of tau. Suvorexant, a dual orexin receptor antagonist, was previously shown to acutely reduce the ratio of phosphorylated tau-181 to unphosphorylated tau-181 (pT181/T181) in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). However, its effects on plasma pT181/T181 and broader proteomic networks remain unknown. Here, we conducted a secondary analysis of samples from a randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 25 participants receiving placebo or suvorexant 20 mg. Paired CSF and plasma samples were collected longitudinally via indwelling intrathecal and intravenous catheters. Plasma AD biomarkers were quantified by mass spectrometry, and proteomic profiling was performed using the SomaScan 11K assay on samples from hours 0, 16, and 24. Suvorexant increased pT181/T181 in plasma while decreasing it in CSF between 08:00-16:00. At the pathway level, suvorexant increased the extracellular abundance of proteins associated with translation, RNA binding, cytoskeletal organization and endosomal sorting, centrally at 16 hours and peripherally at 24 hours, and decreased cytokine and receptor-ligand signaling and serine protease activity in plasma at 24 hours. As plasma biomarkers are increasingly used for clinical screening and trial enrollment, factors that influence their levels, including medications, must be identified and their effects on interpretation understood.

Phenome-wide and proteomic analysis of APOE alleles across different ancestries in the All of Us Research Program

Mejia Garcia, A.; Valderrama-Carmona, P.; Zheng, T. M.; Wang, Z.; Tsao, H. M.; Jung, J.; Orozco, C. A.; Su, C.-Y.; Zhou, S.

BackgroundApolipoprotein E (APOE) {varepsilon}4 is the strongest common genetic risk factor for Alzheimers disease and a determinant of cardiometabolic risk, yet studies of APOE genotypes with disease associations and plasma proteomic signatures have been primarily restricted to European-ancestry cohorts. MethodsWe performed a phenome-wide association study of APOE genotype in 367,757 All of Us participants across six genetic ancestry groups. APOE alleles were tested against 2,632 phenotypes using an ordinal Alzheimers-disease-risk hierarchy ({varepsilon}2/{varepsilon}2 < {varepsilon}2/{varepsilon}3 < {varepsilon}3/{varepsilon}3 < {varepsilon}2/{varepsilon}4 < {varepsilon}3/{varepsilon}4 < {varepsilon}4/{varepsilon}4), adjusted for age, sex at birth, EHR length, and 16 genetic principal components. Sex- and ancestry-stratified analyses used Cochrans Q for heterogeneity. We evaluated {varepsilon}2 and {varepsilon}4 dosage effects on plasma protein levels in 9,132 participants across five ancestries. FindingsWe identified 27 phenotypes associated with APOE, mainly in neurological and lipid-related traits. Five lipid-related traits showed sex heterogeneity, with larger effects in females. Nine associations showed ancestry-specific effects. APOE {varepsilon}4 showed stronger protection against fatty liver disease in East Asians with lack of protection in African ancestry; and larger dementia risk for Europeans. Finally, SNAP25 was identified as positively associated with APOE {varepsilon}4 dosage across ancestries, and CDC42EP1, APOA1 and ITGB5 shown associations with APOE {varepsilon}2/{varepsilon}4 specifically in non-European populations. InterpretationAncestry-heterogeneous APOE effects, particularly for liver and dementia phenotypes, argue for ancestry-aware interpretation of APOE-based risk. Plasma SNAP25 is a candidate ancestry-portable biomarker of APOE-{varepsilon}4 effects, and ancestry-specific proteins nominate mechanisms for the observed phenotypic heterogeneity. Research in context Evidence before this studyThe APOE {varepsilon}4 variant is an established genetic risk factor for hyperlipidemia, Alzheimers disease, dementia, ischemic heart disease and, more recently, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. Prior phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS) of APOE have been conducted largely in European-ancestry cohorts. A recent APOE PheWAS in the All of Us Research Program (n = 181,880) identified 17 Bonferroni-significant phenotypes and preliminary evidence of ancestry heterogeneity for hyperlipidemia and mild cognitive impairment, but statistical power in non-European groups limited systematic characterization of ancestry-specific effects. Recent plasma proteomic studies have described APOE-associated protein signatures shared with neurodegenerative diseases, but these were confined to European-ancestry participants, leaving open whether such signatures generalize across ancestries. Added value of this studyUsing the All of Us v9 release, we approximately doubled the sample size of the prior APOE PheWAS in this cohort (n = 367,757 across six ancestry groups, including [~]146,000 non-European participants), enabling the first well-powered assessment of ancestry heterogeneity of APOE effects. We identify 27 Bonferroni-significant phecodes, 11 novel compared to the previous APOE PheWAS in All of Us -- including vascular dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies, and angina pectoris. We detect significant sex heterogeneity for five lipid-related phecodes and significant ancestry heterogeneity for nine phecodes, including stronger protection against fatty liver and chronic liver disease in East Asian participants and larger dementia risk in European participants. Complementing the phenotypic analysis, we present the first ancestry-stratified plasma proteomic characterization of APOE genotypes (n = 9,132), identifying SNAP25 as a robust APOE-{varepsilon}4-associated protein across ancestries and ancestry-specific signals for IL32, CDC42EP1, APOA1, and ITGB5. Implications of all the available evidenceAPOE-associated risk estimates derived predominantly from European-ancestry data may not transfer directly to other populations, and our findings support incorporating ancestry into the interpretation and calibration of APOE-based risk prediction. The ancestry-heterogeneous protection against liver disease and the parallel APOA1 signal absent in East and South Asian participants together offer a mechanistic hypothesis linking APOE-driven lipid partitioning to hepatic outcomes. Plasma SNAP25 is proposed as an accessible, ancestry-portable biomarker of APOE-{varepsilon}4 effects and Alzheimers disease progression. Ancestry-specific proteins (IL32 and CDC42EP1 in African ancestry, ITGB5 in Admixed American ancestry) constitute potential drivers of ancestry heterogeneity in APOE-related disease risk.

A scalable invariant causal discovery framework reveals direct genetic and clinical determinants of complex human traits

Yin, L.; Liu, M.; Shi, Y.; Qiu, J.; So, H.-C.

Identifying genetic and clinical factors that exert direct causal effects on complex traits is central to precision medicine, yet remains a major challenge. Association-based methods cannot distinguish cause from consequence, and existing causal frameworks are limited by stringent assumptions or computational intractability in high-dimensional settings. Invariant causal prediction (ICP) offers a principled alternative but has seen minimal application in genetic epidemiology due to exponential complexity. Here, we introduce the Invariant Generalized Covariance Measure (I-GCM), a scalable framework that integrates ICP principles with machine learning to identify direct causal variables. I-GCM combines PC-simple screening with a generalized covariance measure (GCM) computed via gradient-boosted trees to test invariance across environments, capturing both linear and nonlinear dependencies. In simulations (up to 3,000 variables, 50,000 samples), I-GCM achieved PPV of [~]0.84-1.00 and specificity [&ge;]0.99, outperforming PC-simple by 0.21-0.29 in precision with comparable sensitivity. Applying I-GCM to the UK Biobank, we identified direct causal genes and clinical risk factors for COVID-19 susceptibility and severity and for HDL and triglyceride levels, with many findings supported by existing literature and pathway enrichment. Notably, JAK1 was identified as a direct causal gene for severe COVID-19, consistent with clinical use of the JAK1-targeting therapy baricitinib. To our knowledge, this is among the first applications of invariance-based causal discovery to genetic epidemiology at a biobank scale. By bridging statistical causality and translational medicine, I-GCM offers a scalable, interpretable, and high-precision framework for mechanistic discovery and therapeutic target prioritization.

Divergent Causes of Death After Ischemic and Hemorrhagic Stroke in the Reperfusion Era: A Contemporary Cohort Study

Kadiyala, V.; Simpkins, A. N.; Chugh, H.; Czarny, R.; Uy-Evanado, A.; Bosson, N.; Tatonetti, N.; Reinier, K.; Chugh, S. S.

BackgroundContemporary data on long-term, cause-specific mortality among stroke survivors are sparse, and much of the existing evidence derives from studies performed before the modern reperfusion era. We sought to characterize long-term cause-specific mortality among stroke survivors to discharge (differentiated by stroke subtype) when compared to in-hospital stroke decedents treated under contemporary standards of care. MethodsUsing a hybrid billing-code/imaging and large language model-assisted chart-review pipeline, we identified adults hospitalized for ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke in the Observational Study of Cardiac Arrest Risk (OSCAR) cohort (Cedars-Sinai Health System, Los Angeles) between 2017 and 2020, followed through June 30, 2025. Cause of death was determined and classified into 10 categories. ResultsAmong 1,344 stroke patients (mean age 74.0 {+/-} 14.9 years; ischemic n=1,036 [77.1%], hemorrhagic n=308 [22.9%]), 203 (15.1%) did not survive the index stroke. Of the 1,141 discharged survivors, 625 (54.8%) died over a median follow-up of 5.2 years. Cerebrovascular causes predominated during index stroke hospitalization (36.0%) but accounted for only 15.5% of post-stroke discharge deaths. Among ischemic stroke survivors, cardiac disease was the leading cause of death (31.6%), with sudden cardiac arrest alone accounting for 10.9% of deaths. Among hemorrhagic stroke survivors, cancer (38.9%) was the leading cause of death. Despite these divergent cause-of-death profiles, overall survival did not differ significantly by subtype (median 5.5 vs 5.8 years; P=0.90). ConclusionsIn this contemporary, reperfusion-era cohort, causes of death shifted from cerebrovascular predominance in the acute phase to heart disease/cardiac arrest after ischemic stroke, and malignancy after hemorrhagic stroke. These findings argue for development of subtype-specific post-stroke surveillance, including structured longitudinal cardiac and sudden-death risk assessment for ischemic stroke survivors.
