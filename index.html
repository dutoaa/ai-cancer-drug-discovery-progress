Variantscape: Retrospective Study of Large Language Model-Based Extraction of Variant-Treatment-Cancer Associations from Published Oncology Abstracts

Wosny, M.; Blindu, A. S.; Boesch, M.; Peres, T.; Niederhauser, T.; Fruh, M.; Rothermundt, C.; Hastings, J.

ObjectivesPrecision oncology relies on accurate interpretation of tumour-detected gene variants, to guide personalised treatment decisions. However, interpretation of variants in context requires extensive information that is often buried within unstructured biomedical literature and obscured by inconsistent nomenclature, making manual retrieval labour-intensive and prone to omissions. In this study, our aim was to evaluate the potential of large language models to extract variant-treatment-cancer associations at scale from published abstracts. DesignWe developed Variantscape, a large-scale, automated pipeline and open-access web tool. It integrates traditional natural language processing methods with state-of-the-art large language models to extract, standardise, and analyse co-associations between genetic variants, cancer types, and therapeutic interventions from published biomedical abstracts. ResultsFrom over 3.7 million abstracts published between January 2014 and October 2026, 886,195 articles containing gene names were eligible for extraction. Among these, 37,895 (4.3%) mentioned a variant, cancer type, and therapeutic agent together, covering 11,985 unique variants across 197 cancer types and 547 therapeutic agents. LLM verification against the abstract text confirmed at least one association in 35,224 papers, giving a literature-derived graph of 10,571 nodes and 44,644 verified edges. Canonical alterations in well-studied cancers (e.g., BRAF V600E in melanoma) were strongly linked to established treatments, while several rare variants also emerged with high-confidence literature support. ConclusionsBy applying large language models to biomedical literature, Variantscape enables scalable, context-aware extraction of trilateral variant-treatment-cancer relationships. This approach supports early evidence synthesis/hypothesis generation, highlights underrecognized or rare associations for MTB preparation, and offers a practical resource for accelerating discovery and supporting precision oncology research and translation. FundingThis study was funded by the School of Medicine at the University of St. Gallen in Switzerland under grant number 2300380. Article SummaryO_ST_ABSStrengths and limitations of this studyC_ST_ABS[bullet] Variantscape combines traditional natural language processing with large language models to enable scalable, automated extraction of variant-cancer-treatment relationships from over 3.7 million biomedical abstracts, substantially reducing the manual search burden inherent to existing molecular tumour board workflows. [bullet]The pipeline applies large language model-based inference to standardise inconsistent genetic nomenclature across the literature, improving the comparability and reliability of extracted associations that would otherwise be obscured by terminological variation. [bullet]Variantscape is designed as a continuously updatable, open-access resource, meaning it can incorporate newly published evidence without requiring repeated manual intervention. [bullet]The pipeline relies solely on abstract-level text rather than full-text articles, which may limit the depth and granularity of extracted associations, as key methodological details, variant context, and nuanced clinical findings are frequently reported only within the body of published papers. [bullet]Automated extraction using large language models introduces a risk of misattribution of variant-cancer-treatment relationships, and without systematic manual validation of outputs, the precision of identified associations at scale remains difficult to fully characterise.

Alzheimers disease and psychiatric polygenic risk are disease-specific and independent of plasma biomarkers in a real-world young-onset dementia cohort.

Bhalala, O. G.; Longfei, W.; Gerring, Z. F.; Han, J.; Kang, M. J.; Trounson, B. O.; Loi, S. M.; Velakoulis, D.; Eratne, D.; Bahlo, M.; The MiND Study Group,

Background: Young onset dementia is frequently misdiagnosed, particularly where neurodegenerative and primary psychiatric disorders (PPD) overlap. Plasma biomarkers measure current pathology, whereas polygenic risk scores (PRS) capture lifelong genetic risk. Whether PRS derived from late-onset and population samples transfer to young-onset presentations, and how they relate to plasma biomarkers, is unknown. Methods: We studied 687 participants of European ancestry from the Markers in Neuropsychiatric Disorders (MiND) Study, a prospective young-onset neuropsychiatry cohort: 195 healthy controls (HC), 283 PPD, 55 Alzheimers disease (AD) and 154 non-AD neurodegenerative disease (oNDG). PRS for AD, a cross-disorder psychiatric factor (PSY), frontotemporal dementia, Lewy body dementia and Parkinsons disease were computed with PRS CS, excluding the APOE locus. Plasma NfL, GFAP and p-tau217 were available for 332 participants. Models were adjusted for age, sex and APOE e4 dose. Results: Relative to HC, the AD PRS was associated with AD (OR 1.69 per SD, 95% CI 1.17-2.45) but not PPD (0.95, 0.77-1.16). The PSY PRS showed the reciprocal pattern (PPD 1.29, 1.05-1.58; AD 1.00, 0.72-1.40). No PRS was associated with any plasma protein. In distinguishing PPD from oNDG, NfL was the strongest discriminator (OR 2.15, 1.62-2.83). The AD PRS was also associated with oNDG (1.38, 1.11-1.71), without improving discrimination (AUC 0.764 for NfL alone versus 0.773 combined). Conclusion: In young onset presentations, AD and psychiatric polygenic risk are disease-specific and independent of plasma biomarkers of neurodegeneration. Their combined diagnostic value requires evaluation in larger, longitudinal pre-clinical cohorts.

Which Attention Helps? A Slide-Level Ablation of Attention-Augmented CNNs for Serous Ovarian Carcinoma Grading

Rani, A.; Mishra, S.

BackgroundDistinguishing high-grade (HGSC) from low-grade serous carcinoma (LGSC) is clinically consequential yet difficult, as the subtypes share a serous morphology but differ in prognosis and treatment. Prior deep-learning studies report near-perfect accuracy, but often under patch-level evaluation that leaks slide information between training and testing. MethodsWe present a leakage-controlled, slide-level ablation of attention-augmented CNNs for this binary task on the UBC-OCEAN cohort (259 whole-slide images; 217 HGSC, 42 LGSC). Under an identical protocol with five-fold slide-grouped cross-validation, Macenko stain normalisation, and slide-level pooling, we compared a from-scratch backbone with no attention, Squeeze-and-Excitation, channel-only CBAM and full CBAM, a lightweight CBAM variant, and an ImageNet-pretrained MobileNet with a differential-attention module. A pathology foundation model (Phikon) with attention-based multiple-instance aggregation was evaluated as a modern benchmark. Results are reported across three training seeds, with decision thresholds selected within folds, and compared using DeLong and McNemar tests with Holm correction and bootstrap confidence intervals. ResultsThe pretrained model performed best (AUC 0.862 {+/-} 0.005, MCC 0.460) and was the most stable across seeds. It significantly outranked both the Squeeze-and-Excitation model (DeLong, Holm p = 0.002) and the no-attention baseline (p = 0.019). Full CBAM also outranked Squeeze-and-Excitation (p = 0.010), but adding the spatial-attention branch to channel attention produced no detectable change (0.824 to 0.822; p = 0.50), and the lightweight model did not match the deep one once training variance was accounted for. The foundation-model benchmark reached AUC 0.834 {+/-} 0.018, below the pretrained MobileNet. A colour-only classifier reached AUC 0.66, indicating part of the separability is attributable to stain and colour rather than morphology. ConclusionUnder leakage-free evaluation, performance is more modest than commonly reported. Transfer learning and the avoidance of a weak channel-only configuration produced the clearest gains, while finer attention design, network size and a pathology-specific foundation model produced no detectable improvement. With only 42 LGSC slides the task remains underpowered for finer distinctions, and most architectural differences could not be resolved.
